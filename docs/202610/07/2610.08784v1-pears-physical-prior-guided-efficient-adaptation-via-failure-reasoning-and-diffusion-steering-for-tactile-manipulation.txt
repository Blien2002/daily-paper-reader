Abstract — Pretrained robotic policies can suffer sub-stantial performance degradation under out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions encountered during deployment, motivat-ing post-training through real-world interaction. However, reinforcement-learning (RL)-based post-training typically re-quires substantial environment interactions, a burden that is especially significant in manipulation, where each trial can be slow, costly, or destructive. Therefore, we present PEARS, a physics-prior-guided hybrid RL framework for sample-efficient online adaptation of pretrained policies with tactile feedback. After each episode, its physics-guided force reasoning (PFR) module uses physical priors encoded in a vision–language model (VLM) to diagnose failures from the visual outcome and tactile interaction history and update task-appropriate contact-force bounds. A high-frequency hybrid force–position controller then enforces these bounds during contact. Complementarily, tactile-conditioned diffusion steering reinforcement learning adjusts the latent noise of the frozen flow-matching policy to correct errors in free-space motion and contact timing without updating the base model. In simulation, PEARS improves success rates by 12.4–37.4 percentage points over the strongest per-task baselines. PEARS also reduces the number of interaction episodes required for a certain success threshold by up to 53.2% relative to the fastest baseline. In real-world experiments, PEARS achieves success rates of 95% on Whiteboard Erasing and 90% on Pipette Liquid Aspiration. I. I NTRODUCTION 

Large-scale pretrained vision–language–action (VLA) models [1], [2] have recently emerged as an important paradigm for robotic manipulation, achieving remarkable progress in task generalization across diverse real-world environments. For contact-rich tasks, such as surface wiping and grasping fragile objects, vision–tactile–language–action (VTLA) models [3] extend this paradigm by incorporating tactile feedback to sense and regulate physical interactions with the environment. However, pretrained VLA and VTLA policies can perform poorly during deployment when scene configurations, object properties, or contact dynamics dif-fer from those encountered during training. Such out-of-distribution (OOD) shifts can lead to positioning errors, mistimed contact, or inappropriate interaction forces [4]. Reinforcement learning (RL) post-training with tactile feedback [5], [6] provides a direct route for adapting pretrained policies to the target deployment environment through autonomous interaction. However, traditional RL-based algorithms demand substantial interaction with the en-vironment, which can be time-inefficient and high-cost. For example, a failed online interaction in an industrial operation or chemical laboratory procedure may damage equipment or compromise the experimental setup. This interaction cost is compounded by conventional online RL algorithms, which arXiv:2610.08784v1 [cs.RO] 6 Oct 2026 often rely on stochastic trial-and-error exploration and must visit a sufficiently diverse set of task-relevant states before effective behaviors can be learned. Therefore, how to develop an efficient real-world RL fine-tuning method for contact-rich manipulation with tactile feedback is still an open-problem. To this end, we introduce PEARS , a physics-prior-guided framework for interaction-efficient real-world adap-tation in tactile manipulation. Its physics-guided force rea-soning (PFR) module uses physical priors encoded in a vision–language model (VLM) to reason about episode-level failures and guide contact-force adaptation, rather than relying solely on random RL exploration. After each episode, the PFR module jointly interprets the visual outcome and measured interaction forces to diagnose the failure cause and update task-specific contact-force bounds. During contact, a high-frequency hybrid force–position controller enforces these bounds along the force-control axes while maintaining position tracking in the remaining directions. Complemen-tarily, tactile-conditioned DSRL [7] steers the frozen policy through latent-noise adaptation to correct positioning and contact-timing errors without updating the base model. By separating force adaptation through PFR from latent policy steering, PEARS reduces its reliance on costly real-world trial-and-error exploration. We evaluated PEARS on three simulated tasks and two real-world manipulation tasks. Experimental results demon-strate that PEARS achieves superior performance, signifi-cantly outperforming representative policy adaptation base-lines. Our contributions are threefold:  

> •

We present PEARS , an interaction-efficient online fine-tuning framework that combines noise-space steering with VLM-based force reasoning using tactile sensing.  

> •

We develop the PFR module , which uses physical priors encoded in VLMs to diagnose failure causes and adapt task-specific contact-force bounds, reducing interaction numbers required for policy fine-tuning.  

> •

We conduct extensive experiments across simulated and real-world contact-rich manipulation tasks, demonstrat-ing the effectiveness and efficiency of PEARS. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Tactile-Aware Visuomotor Policies 

Recent studies have increasingly incorporated tactile sens-ing into visuomotor policies. These approaches employ tactile encoders [8], [9], [10], [11] cross-modal align-ment [12], [13], [14] or auxiliary tactile supervision [15] to leverage learned tactile representations to guide ac-tion generation. [16], [17], [3], [18] further pretrain vi-sion–tactile–language–action (VTLA) models on tactile datasets collected across diverse tasks and heterogeneous tactile sensor configurations, enabling VTLA models to learn more unified and transferable tactile representations. 

B. Reactive Tactile Policies for Contact-Rich Manipulation 

Contact-rich manipulation requires high-frequency re-sponses to tactile feedback. FOAR [19] uses predicted con-tact states to dynamically modulate vision-force fusion and trigger local action corrections. RDP [20] introduces a slow-fast architecture that pairs slow action chunk generation with a fast tactile-conditioned decoder, enabling within-chunk cor-rections. ForcePolicy [21] and ForceVLA2 [22] employ force feedback to perform high-frequency hybrid force-position control. This reactive design has been extended to tactile-aware VLA models. VLA-Touch [23] uses tactile feedback to support both semantic replanning and action refinement. AT-VLA [24] separates slow vision-language reasoning from fast tactile control. FAVLA [25] adaptively adjusts the action expert invocation frequency based on predicted force varia-tions. UniTacVLA [26] combines predicted and measured tactile signals to apply high-frequency corrections to low-frequency action chunks. 

C. Reinforcement Learning for Tactile Policy Adaptation 

Beyond behavior cloning (BC), RL can further improve tactile policies through autonomous interaction with the environment. VLA-RL [27] optimizes autoregressive VLAs through trajectory-level RL. SimpleVLA-RL [28] presents an efficient and scalable RL framework tailored to VLAs.

Rather than directly updating VLA parameters, DSRL [7] treats the initial noise of a diffusion or flow-matching policy as the RL latent action space and learns a noise policy, enabling effective autonomous policy im-provement in the real world. RL Token [30] compresses task-relevant internal VLA representations into a compact RL token and trains a lightweight actor-critic head on this representation to refine actions while anchoring behavior to the base VLA, enabling sample-efficient online adaptation on real robots. 2) PFR Module: After each episode, PFR queries the VLM with the final contact-phase image, the measured force trajectory, and the force bounds used during execution. The VLM assesses whether the interaction force was insufficient, excessive, or adequate, while distinguishing force-related failures from positioning, trajectory, or force-tracking errors. These diagnoses provide evidence for estimating the task-specific force bounds. The diagnosis determines whether and how the force bounds are updated. An insufficient-force diagnosis shifts the corresponding interval toward a larger force, whereas an excessive-force diagnosis shifts it toward a smaller force. When the evidence is indeterminate, the current estimate is retained. Likewise, if the failure is attributed to positioning or trajectory execution, the force bounds remain unchanged. This outcome-conditioned update has a direct task-dependent interpretation. If visible marks remain on a whiteboard after wiping, PFR increases the admissible normal-force range. If a fragile fruit is crushed during harvesting, it reduces the grasp-force range; if the fruit remains attached to the stem, it instead increases the pulling-force range. To make PFR decision cumulative rather than myopic, we compress the complete interaction history into the VLM con-text. The history records the force bounds of each episode, whether the episode succeeded or failed, the diagnosed failure cause, and the resulting update. Consequently, each new estimate is conditioned on both the latest multimodal evidence and the outcomes of all preceding interactions.

A. Experimental Setup 1) Evaluation Tasks: We consider three simulation tasks: Thin Sheet Transfer, Bottle Cap Twisting, and Fragile Fruit Picking. In Thin Sheet Transfer, the robot must transport a fragile thin sheet to the target and regulating its grasp force to avoid crushing it. The object position is randomized within a 

0.2 m ×0.2 m region, and the maximum allowable interaction force is 1.1–1.6 N . In Bottle Cap Twisting, the robot must tighten a bottle cap and establish an appropriate normal force between the cap and the bottle after tightening. This task evaluates whether the PFR module can infer an appropriate interaction torque. The object position is randomized within a 0.1 m × 0.1 m region, and the target torque range for successful completion is 0.35 –0.50 N m . In Fragile Fruit Picking, the robot must detach a raspberry from its vine. It must apply a gentle grasp to avoid crushing the fruit while exerting sufficient pulling force for detachment. The object position is randomized by up to ±[8 , 10 , 12] mm along the 

x-, y-, and z-axes, respectively. The crushing-force threshold is 3.2 N , and the minimum force required for detachment is 

0.8 N . The preset open-loop pulling offset is zero. For real-world evaluation, we consider two tasks: White-board Erasing and Pipette Liquid Aspiration. In Whiteboard Erasing, the robot must learn to apply an appropriate contact force between the eraser and the whiteboard to remove the marker traces. In Pipette Liquid Aspiration, the robot must regulate the fingertip contact force on the pipette: the force must be sufficient to lift the pipette while remaining low enough to prevent the liquid inside from being expelled. DPPO [34] uses a PPO-style objective to update the diffusion policy. PEARS , Residual RL , and DSRL combine a tactile-conditioned actor. For all RL algorithms, we use a sparse terminal reward of +10 for a successful episode and −10 

for a failed episode, with a discount factor of γ = 0 .9. In simulation, success or failure is determined automatically by the simulator, whereas real-world outcomes are labeled manually by a human operator. 

B. Comparison with Baselines ( Q1 )

Each adaptive method used three 100-episode online runs, followed by 100 test trials per run. Final-policy success rate (SR) is reported as mean ± sample SD. Let SR e ∈

[0 , 1] denote trailing-10 online success averaged across runs (Fig. 3). Normalized area under the learning curve (nAUC) is the mean of SR e for e = 10 , . . . , 100 , expressed as a percentage; E80 is the first episode with SR e ≥ 0.8 (“/” if unreached). nAUC measures overall online performance, while a smaller E80 indicates faster attainment of a high success rate. Force-related failure rate is the percentage of all episodes ending in task-specific force or contact failures, pooled across runs. PEARS achieved the highest SR on Thin Sheet Transfer, Bottle Cap Twisting, and Fragile Fruit Picking: 94 .7% ,

87 .7% , and 77 .7% (Table I). These exceed the strongest per-task baselines by 12.4, 19.7, and 37.4 percentage points. It also led in nAUC ( 94 .7% , 90 .1% , and 73 .4% ), indicating higher success throughout online adaptation. The largest SR gain occurs on Fragile Fruit Picking, which requires coordinated grasping and pulling without damaging the fruit. PEARS reached E80 at episodes 10, 22, and 25. On Thin Sheet Transfer, episode 10 is the earliest evaluable window, compared with 12 for the fastest baseline, Residual RL. On Bottle Cap Twisting, Residual RL required 47 episodes, so PEARS reduced interactions to the threshold by 53 .2% . No baseline reached 80% on Fragile Fruit Picking within 100 episodes. Higher success was accompanied by the lowest force-related failure rates ( 2.0% , 10 .3% , and 3.3% ), compared with 9.7% , 20 .3% , and 36 .3% for the strongest SR baselines. The mean waiting time for a VLM response was 26.8 s, which is acceptable because this wait can overlap with other steps, such as environment resets.

D. Real-world Experiment ( Q3 )

For each real-world task, DSRL and PEARS are allocated 40 episodes of online interaction. We then evaluate each adapted policy and the frozen base policy over 20 test trials (Table III). In Whiteboard Erasing, sufficient normal force is needed to remove marker traces, whereas excessive force can dislodge the eraser from the gripper. The base policy succeeds in 11 /20 trials; its failures comprise three wiping misalignments, two contact-timing errors that leave residual marks, and four cases of insufficient force. DSRL improves success to 15 /20 , with two misalignment failures and three insufficient-force failures. PEARS reaches 19 /20 ,with the sole failure caused by misalignment. In Pipette Liquid Aspiration, any liquid leakage during transfer counts as a failure. The base policy succeeds in 8/20 trials, with four initial grasping misalignments, six cases of leakage during transfer, and two failures during final dispensing. DSRL reaches 12 /20 , with two grasping misalignments, five cases of liquid expulsion during transfer, and one dispensing failure. PEARS succeeds in 18 /20 , with both failures caused by initial grasping misalignment. These results suggest that DSRL alone struggles to learn precise force regulation within 40 episodes, particularly for liquid transfer. The absence of force-related failures with PEARS in these tests supports the benefit of using PFR to adapt force bounds from tactile feedback and enforcing them through low-level force control. VI. C ONCLUSION & F UTURE WORK 

We presented PEARS, a framework for rapid post-training of pretrained policies that combines PFR with traditional DSRL. PFR uses tactile feedback and visual outcomes to diagnose failure causes and adapt contact-force bounds, which are enforced by a low-level controller, while DSRL refines the behavior of the frozen base policy. Across three simulation tasks, PEARS improved success rates by 12.4– 37.4 percentage points over the strongest baseline for each task, with faster online adaptation and fewer force-related failures. In real-world tasks, PEARS achieved 19 /20 and 

18 /20 successful test trials on Whiteboard Erasing and Pipette Liquid Aspiration, respectively. Currently, PFR provides corrective guidance only for failures caused by inappropriate force; other errors still rely on conventional RL for correction. Future work will explore integrating failure reasoning more broadly into RL to guide exploration and policy updates beyond force regulation.
