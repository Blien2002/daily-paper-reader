Abstract — Human videos offer scalable manipulation data, but the embodiment gap between human hands and robot manipulators limits their direct use. Existing video-editing methods replace hands with rendered robots, yet inaccu-rate interaction reconstruction and compositing can produce inconsistent grasps and implausible robot–object occlusions. We address these failures from two complementary physi-cal aspects: interaction geometry and scene visibility. First, an interaction-aware contact reconstruction module combines hand–object segmentation with mesh-level contact prediction to recover dense 3D contacts, then converts them into tem-porally stabilized grasps for parallel-jaw grippers. Second, a depth-aware compositing module uses scene and robot depth to enforce physically consistent robot–object occlusions. The resulting videos preserve the interaction structure of human demonstrations in a robot-compatible form and are co-trained with robot demonstrations. Using identical human videos and robot data, we compare against robot-only training and the original Masquerade pipeline. Across four RoboTwin tasks and two Diffusion Policy visual encoders, our method achieves the highest average success rates, with especially strong gains un-der out-of-distribution scene variation. Real-world deployment further shows that the proposed co-training approach improves robustness to visual distractors when the task geometry is ob-servable, while performance on depth-sensitive grasps remains limited by the single-camera setup. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

The advancement in robot learning has increasingly bene-fited from large and diverse demonstration datasets [2], [3]. However, collecting robot demonstrations at scale remains fundamentally expensive because each trajectory requires physical hardware, environment interaction, and often human teleoperation or supervision [4], [5]. Consequently, robot datasets remain far smaller and less diverse than the image, text, and video corpora that have driven progress in large 

> †Corresponding author and project lead.

language models [6], [7] and video generation models [8]. This data bottleneck limits the diversity of interactions avail-able during training and, in turn, the ability of manipulation policies to generalize across objects, tasks, and environments. Human videos offer a compelling alternative source of manipulation experience. Large-scale egocentric video col-lections [9], [10], [11] capture everyday interactions across diverse objects, scenes, and behaviors at a scale that is difficult to reproduce with physical robots. In principle, these videos could provide robot policies with substantially broader manipulation experience. In practice, however, hu-man demonstrations differ fundamentally from robot demon-strations [12], [13]. Human hands differ from robot manip-ulators in appearance, kinematics, and contact mechanisms, while human videos typically lack robot-compatible action labels. This embodiment gap prevents conventional robot learning pipelines from directly treating human videos as robot demonstrations. Recent work has begun to bridge this gap by con-verting human demonstrations into robot-compatible visual data [12], [13]. In particular, Phantom [14] and Masquer-ade [1] introduce a video-editing paradigm that reconstructs human hand motion from egocentric video, removes the human hand through inpainting, and renders a robot manip-ulator along the recovered trajectory, as shown in the middle of Fig. 1. The resulting robotized videos reduce the visual embodiment discrepancy and can be incorporated into robot policy training. This offers a promising route for scaling robot learning with abundant human video. However, successful human-to-robot conversion requires more than replacing the visual embodiment. A manipulation demonstration is informative because it encodes a physically coherent interaction between the agent, object, and scene. arXiv:2610.06137v1 [cs.RO] 5 Oct 2026 If the conversion process alters this interaction structure, the edited video may look robot-like while providing in-consistent supervision. This issue is particularly important because human-to-robot editing is a multi-stage process in which errors from hand reconstruction, grasp retargeting, and scene compositing can accumulate. For example, retargeting a robot gripper from hand pose or motion alone does not explicitly preserve where the human contacts the object, and can therefore produce a gripper pose that is kinematically plausible but inconsistent with the demonstrated grasp. Like-wise, naively overlaying a rendered robot onto the edited scene ignores scene geometry and can place gripper pixels in front of object regions that should physically occlude them. Such artifacts create disagreement between robot motion, ob-ject interaction, and visual evidence, weakening the value of the converted video as policy supervision. We therefore argue that the key challenge is not only to reduce the appearance gap between humans and robots, but also to preserve the physical consistency of the demonstrated interaction. To this end, we propose an interaction-faithful human-to-robot video-editing pipeline that addresses physical consis-tency from two complementary aspects: interaction geometry 

and scene visibility . First, we explicitly reconstruct hand– object contact rather than deriving the robot grasp from hand motion alone. Our interaction-aware contact reconstruction combines hand–object segmentation with mesh-level contact prediction to recover dense 3D contact regions on the hu-man hand. We then reduce these contacts to a temporally stabilized thumb–opposing-finger contact pair that can be naturally mapped to a parallel-jaw gripper. Surface-normal constraints further favor contact pairs that correspond to geometrically plausible opposing grasp surfaces. This formu-lation grounds robot retargeting in the demonstrated hand– object interaction itself, rather than only in the reconstructed hand configuration. Second, we introduce depth-aware robot– scene compositing to preserve physically correct visibility. By comparing the predicted depth of the hand-inpainted scene with the rendered robot depth, we identify gripper regions that should lie behind the manipulated object and remove them from the final composite. Together, as shown in the right of Fig. 1, these components preserve both where the robot should interact with the object and how that interaction should appear in the scene. We evaluate the resulting robotized demonstrations by co-training manipulation policies with real robot demonstra-tions. Using the same source human videos and robot training data, we compare our pipeline against robot-only training and the original Masquerade editing pipeline. Experiments span four RoboTwin manipulation tasks and two visual encoders within Diffusion Policy. Our edited demonstrations consis-tently improve policy performance, achieving the highest av-erage success rates across settings. The gains are particularly pronounced under out-of-distribution scene variation, sug-gesting that physically consistent human-video supervision provides useful interaction cues beyond the visual conditions observed in the robot training data. We further validate the approach on a physical robot, where it improves robustness to visual distractors for tasks with sufficiently observable interaction geometry. Results on a depth-sensitive grasp also reveal the limitations imposed by ambiguous geometric cues in a single-camera setup. Our main contributions are:  

> •

We identify interaction consistency as a key requirement for converting human videos into reliable robot-learning supervision.  

> •

We introduce interaction-aware contact reconstruction that recovers dense 3D hand–object contacts and retargets them into stable, geometrically plausible parallel-jaw grasps.  

> •

We propose depth-aware compositing that preserves phys-ically consistent robot–object occlusions in edited videos.  

> •

We show across four RoboTwin tasks, two Diffusion Policy visual encoders, and real-robot experiments that our edited demonstrations improve average performance and robustness to out-of-distribution scene variation. II. R ELATED WORK 

Learning from egocentric human video. Egocentric video offers a scalable source of human manipulation experience from a viewpoint closely aligned with robot observations. Large-scale datasets such as EPIC-KITCHENS [9], HD-EPIC [11], and EgoVerse [10] capture diverse everyday interactions across objects, tasks, and environments. Despite this scale and diversity, directly using such videos for robot learning remains difficult because they depict human hands rather than robot manipulators and generally lack robot-compatible action labels, creating both embodiment and action-space gaps. Several works address these gaps by in-corporating human demonstrations directly into robot policy learning. EgoMimic [15] tracks human hand motion and aligns egocentric human demonstrations with robot data for joint imitation learning. EgoVLA [12] leverages egocentric human video as supervision for vision–language–action mod-els, while EgoScale [13] studies how diverse egocentric data can scale dexterous manipulation learning. These approaches demonstrate that human videos can complement expensive robot-collected trajectories, but require learning mechanisms that explicitly accommodate the mismatch between human and robot embodiments. 

Human-to-robot video editing. A complementary line of work addresses the embodiment gap at the data level by converting human videos into robotized demonstrations be-fore policy training. Phantom [14] explores learning robot manipulation from human videos without requiring robot demonstrations. Masquerade [1] reconstructs human hand motion, removes the human hand through video inpainting, and renders a target robot along the recovered trajectory. The resulting videos visually resemble robot demonstrations and can be co-trained with conventional robot data, providing a direct way to reuse human manipulation experience within standard robot-learning pipelines. Our work follows this data-editing and co-training paradigm, but focuses on a com-plementary problem: whether the converted video preserves the physical interaction encoded in the original demonstra-tion. We address these limitations from two complementary aspects. First, we incorporate explicit hand–object contact geometry when retargeting human grasps to robot grippers. Second, we use depth-based occlusion reasoning when compositing the rendered robot into the scene. Together, these components improve both the physical interaction and its visual con-sistency, producing more faithful robot-learning supervision from the same egocentric source videos. III. A. Problem Formulation and Overview 

We consider the problem of converting egocentric human manipulation videos into robotized demonstrations for policy co-training with conventional robot demonstrations. Given an RGB video sequence V = {It}Tt=1 and a target robot equipped with parallel-jaw grippers, our goal is to recover robot-compatible motion and grasp information and generate an edited video in which the robot replaces the human hands. The conversion should preserve the contact geometry of the human demonstration and the visibility relationships between the rendered gripper and the surrounding scene. B. Interaction-Aware Contact Reconstruction 

Accurate hand–object contact is essential for transferring human manipulation to a robot, as it determines not only when the robot gripper should be activated, but also which re-gions of the human hand provide the most relevant geometric constraints for retargeting. To recover this information from egocentric video, we construct a dense 3D contact represen-tation by combining contact-aware hand–object segmentation with mesh-level contact estimation. As illustrated in Fig. 2, our contact reconstruction pipeline consists of three main stages. First, we obtain dense hand and interacting-object masks by augmenting VISOR [16] with CaRe-Ego [17]. Sec-ond, the centroid of the segmented interacting-object mask is used as an image-space contact cue for LatentAct [18], which predicts a dense contact map over the reconstructed MANO hand mesh. Finally, the predicted contact region is partitioned into thumb and non-thumb regions and reduced to two representative 3D contact points, which provide a compact grasp representation compatible with a parallel-jaw robot gripper. Temporal stabilization and surface-normal filtering are further applied to improve the consistency of the extracted contacts across frames. 

a) Contact-Aware Hand–Object Segmentation.: VI-SOR [16] provides pixel-level annotations for hands and active objects; however, the annotations are temporally sparse and do not directly provide dense 3D hand contact. We aug-ment the VISOR annotations using CaRe-Ego [17], which explicitly models the contact relationship between hands and interacting objects. In particular, CaRe-Ego employs hand features to enhance the representation of interacting objects and decouples contact-centric object features associated with different hands. We use the resulting segmentation to obtain dense hand and interacting-object masks for each frame, denoted by M ht and M ot , respectively. C. Depth-Consistent Robot–Scene Compositing 

After retargeting the recovered human hand trajectory to the robot embodiment, we render the robot into the egocentric scene to generate robotized demonstrations. A key challenge in this step is handling occlusion between the rendered robot gripper and the real manipulated object. Directly overlaying the rendered robot on the raw frame can produce physically implausible images, because object regions that should appear in front of the robot may be incorrectly covered by the rendered gripper. To address this issue, we estimate occlusion using the inpainted video frame rather than the raw egocentric frame. These tasks cover grasping, object adjustment, placement, and dynamic manipulation. The clean and randomized RoboTwin scenes correspond to our ID and OOD settings, respectively. For each task, all settings use the same 50 robot demon-strations. Masquerade and ours augment these demonstra-tions with robotized versions of the same human videos, allowing us to isolate the effect of editing quality while keeping the amount and source of training data fixed. We evaluate Diffusion Policy (DP) [21] with ViT [22] and Swin Transformer [23] visual encoders, denoted DP-ViT and DP-Swin, to test whether the observed trends hold across visual representations. We report task success rates over 100 rollouts for every policy, task, and scene. Relative to Masquerade, the average OOD gains are 22.25 and 15.50 percentage points, whereas the corresponding ID gains are only 2.50 and 2.00 points. C. Ablation Studies 

We ablate the two main components of our editing pipeline: interaction-aware contact reconstruction and depth-based robot occlusion estimation. To keep the comparison controlled and computationally efficient, all ablations use DP-ViT with the same source videos, robot demonstrations, architecture, and training configuration. Each variant re-moves one component while retaining the other and follows the ID/OOD protocol in Sec. IV-B. 

w/o Contact Reconstruction. We replace our contact reconstruction (Sec. III-B) with the original Masquerade grasp representation while retaining the reconstructed hand motion and depth-based occlusion estimation. This variant isolates the contribution of contact-guided grasp retargeting. 

w/o Depth-Based Occlusion. We composite the rendered robot directly onto the hand-inpainted frame, without com-paring the inpainted-scene and rendered-robot depths as in Sec. III-C. Contact reconstruction and retargeting remain unchanged, isolating the contribution of depth-aware com-positing. As shown in Tab. II, removing contact reconstruction reduces average OOD success from 45.75% to 30.25%, despite increasing ID success from 68.00% to 70.75%. Removing depth-based occlusion lowers average success to 66.25%/35.75% in ID/OOD scenes. Both components therefore improve OOD generalization, with contact recon-struction providing the larger contribution and depth-aware compositing providing a complementary gain.
