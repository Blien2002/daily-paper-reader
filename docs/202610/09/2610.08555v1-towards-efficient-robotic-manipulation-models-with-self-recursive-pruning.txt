Abstract — Network pruning can reduce parameter redun-dancy in robotic policies. However, generic pruning criteria are tailored for image recognition tasks and commonly designed to preserve weight magnitude, local reconstruction, or language-model likelihood rather than closed-loop action behavior. Di-rectly applying these pruning algorithms to robotic tasks yields unsatisfactory performance. In this paper, we propose Loss-Conditioned Activation-Moment (LCAM) pruning, a training-free method for unstructured pruning of pre-trained robotic manipulation policies. Specifically, we first rank connections using row-normalized weight contribution, activation moments measured on calibration demonstrations, and the sensitivity of output directions to the action-prediction loss. We further design a self-recursive coarse-to-fine procedure: importance is recalibrated after each nested coarse pruning stage, while held-out offline action distortion guides fine-grained budget allocation after a sparsity knee. Our algorithm is free from costly recovery training and simulator rollouts after pruning. Experiments on three LIBERO suites with competitive robotic policies, together with evaluations on OpenVLA, show that LCAM attains competitive performance across a broad range of pruning ratios. Notably, on LIBERO-Object with OpenVLA, our LCAM achieves 84 .0% success at 50% unstructured prun-ing, retaining over 90% of the dense policy’s success rate. Promising results on real-world robotic ping pong further demonstrate the effectiveness of our pruning algorithm. 

I. INTRODUCTION Recent advances in robot learning have produced action policies that achieve strong manipulation performance across diverse manipulation tasks [1]–[9]. However, modern robotic manipulation policies typically rely on high-capacity percep-tion and cumbersome policy networks, leading to substantial storage and inference costs. This motivates the compression of robotic policies, with the objective of reducing parameter redundancy while maximally preserving the ultimate perfor-mance. We focus on unstructured pruning, which removes individual weights to induce sparsity in pretrained policies. Classical magnitude pruning and recent one-shot methods, such as SparseGPT and Wanda, have demonstrated effective unstructured sparsification for pretrained image recognition and language models [10]–[12]. These pruning methods typically employ classification accuracy, perplexity, or re-construction errors as pruning criteria. However, directly applying these criteria to robotic manipulation policies can be challenging. The challenge stems from the closed-loop nature of robotic manipulation. Unlike language modeling, where pruning quality can often be assessed using token-level likelihood or layer-wise reconstruction errors, robotic policies are ulti-mately evaluated by the task success rate during closed-loop execution. Small action deviations introduced by pruning can accumulate during closed-loop execution and thus yield degraded manipulation performance [13]. Moreover, robotic demonstrations are typically scarce and task-specific, while modern policies combine heterogeneous perception, state, language, and action-generation modules that differ markedly in their sensitivity to parameter removal. To mitigate such degradation, recent robotic policy compression and post-pruning recovery methods often couple structural reduction with additional optimization or test-time correction [14]– [17]. These procedures either introduce additional training cost or require intervention in test-time. Consequently, prun-ing objectives based on generic activation statistics or local reconstruction fidelity can not fully reflect the task-relevant behavior of robotic policies under closed-loop execution. To address the aforementioned challenges, we propose 

Loss-Conditioned Activation-Moment (LCAM) pruning, a training-free framework for sparsifying pretrained robotic policies. At the connection level, LCAM ranks connections by combining row-normalized weight contribution, activa-tion evidence on calibration demonstrations, and action-loss sensitivity. High-score connections are retained, whereas low-score connections are removed. At the allocation level, LCAM adopts a self-recursive coarse-to-fine pruning pro-cedure. Starting from the dense policy, each subsequent stage begins with the previously pruned policy, recalibrates connection importance on the resulting sparse model, and removes only redundant connections. The procedure auto-matically transitions from coarse to fine-grained pruning when offline action distortion on validation demonstrations exhibits a sharp marginal increase, indicating a sparsity knee. It then refines the remaining pruning budget using smaller increments to preserve action behavior at high sparsity. Note that LCAM requires neither post-pruning retraining nor simulator rollouts during pruning. A. Network Pruning 

Network pruning aims to identify and remove redundant parameters while preserving the task performance of a pre-trained model. Magnitude pruning is a classical and widely used baseline that removes parameters according to their absolute weight values [10], [20]. Despite its simplicity, magnitude pruning ranks parameters solely by their absolute values, without accounting for input-dependent activation statistics or action-loss sensitivity. More recent pruning methods incorporate additional in-formation, such as loss sensitivity, activation statistics, or layer-wise reconstruction, to estimate parameter importance. Taylor-based pruning estimates the loss change induced by removing a parameter group using first- or second-order Taylor expansions [21]. Although originally devel-oped for structured pruning, its loss-based saliency principle also motivates parameter-level importance criteria. For large pretrained Transformers, SparseGPT formulates pruning as a layer-wise reconstruction problem and uses approximate second-order information to enable accurate one-shot prun-ing [11]. Wanda provides a simpler training-free alterna-tive by combining weight magnitude with input activation statistics, without updating the retained weights [12]. Pruner-Zero further uses genetic programming to search symbolic pruning metrics over weights, activations, gradients, and mathematical operators [22]. AutoPrune takes a different approach by using an LLM-guided search procedure to automatically discover pruning metrics, together with a skew-aware dynamic sparsity allocation strategy for large language models [23]. These methods were primarily developed and evaluated for image classification or language modeling, where prun-ing is commonly guided by proxy quantities such as loss sensitivity, activation statistics, or layer-wise reconstruction, and evaluated using task-level metrics such as classification accuracy or perplexity. Whether these criteria can reliably preserve task-relevant action behavior when transferred to robotic action policies remains an empirical question, since the optimization objectives and evaluation protocols differ substantially across these settings. 

B. Model Compression and Pruning for Robotic Policies 

Recent work has explored the compression of robotic policies. LightDP compresses diffusion policies through a unified pruning-and-retraining pipeline and combines net-work compression with consistency distillation to reduce the number of denoising steps [14]. RLRC applies structured pruning to vision-language-action models and recovers per-formance through supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning [15]. Shallow-π reduces the depth of flow-based VLA models and trains the compressed model through knowledge distillation [16]. These approaches couple struc-tural compression with additional optimization or distillation to preserve policy performance. Recent work has also investigated post-pruning recovery without additional training. GLUESTICK recovers a struc-turally pruned vision-language-action model by approximat-ing the weight difference between the dense and pruned mod-els with low-rank components, which are added as layer-wise corrections during inference [17]. Unlike these approaches, LCAM constructs pruning masks without recovery training, distillation, or inference-time correction. III. When the criterion is triggered, the current coarse candidate is rejected and the policy is restored to the pre-ceding accepted mask, which defines the safe boundary for fine allocation. Starting from the safe boundary, the fine stage allocates the remaining pruning budget in small increments, e.g., 1% .For each feasible functional group, we construct a candidate mask by removing its lowest-cost remaining nonzero connec-tions. All candidates are evaluated on the same fixed Dval ,and we permanently apply the group that yields the small-est action-distortion increase per newly pruned connection. LCAM therefore determines which connections are removed within each candidate group, while offline action distortion determines which group is selected for the next fine-grained pruning increment. All accepted updates remove additional connections only. The resulting masks remain nested throughout both stages. The procedure performs no gradient-based parameter opti-mization, recovery training, distillation, simulator rollout, or test-success-guided mask selection. IV. EXPERIMENTS 

A. Experimental Setup a) Benchmarks and Policies: We evaluate pruning algo-rithms on three LIBERO suites, including LIBERO-Spatial, LIBERO-Object, and LIBERO-Goal. We use MATE as the primary robotic manipulation policy backbone [19]. We additionally evaluate LCAM on OpenVLA, a representative open-source vision-language-action model for robotic ma-nipulation [7]. Specifically, we use the official OpenVLA-7B checkpoint fine-tuned on LIBERO-Object and evaluate it on the corresponding LIBERO-Object suite. The observation modalities, language instructions, image resolution, action representation, and policy architectures follow the original training and evaluation protocols of the respective models. 

b) Data protocol: For each backbone, we use disjoint demonstration streams for a calibration set Dcal and a held-out allocation set Dval . The former is used to estimate LCAM importance statistics, whereas the latter is used only to measure offline action distortion during coarse-to-fine allocation. Therefore, neither test trajectories nor task success rates are used to select pruning masks, transition points, pruning allocations, or hyperparameters. 

c) Pruning scope and configuration: Unless otherwise stated, we evaluate target eligible-weight pruning ratios of 

10% , 20% , 30% , 40% , 50% , and 60% . We apply unstructured pruning to both MATE and OpenVLA under their respective eligible parameter scopes. For each backbone, the eligible scope is fixed before pruning and kept identical across all compared methods. Backbone-specific pruning scopes and implementation details are provided in the appendix. 

d) Implementation details: All experiments are con-ducted on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU with 48 GB of memory. Unless otherwise stated, LCAM uses 16 calibration batches and 16 held-out validation batches. The coarse and fine pruning increments are 10% and 1% ,respectively, and the knee-sensitivity multiplier is set to 

β = 2 .0.

B. Baselines 

We compare LCAM with representative training-free un-structured pruning baselines. Random pruning removes eli-gible weights uniformly at random and serves as a lower-bound reference. Magnitude pruning ranks weights by their absolute values. Wanda combines weight magnitude with input activation statistics. Taylor pruning estimates parameter saliency using first-order action-loss sensitivity. We further include a Pruner-Zero-style gradient-saliency baseline. Auto-Prune uses the pruning criterion automatically discovered by its original search procedure. These baselines cover random, weight-based, activation-aware, loss-sensitive, and automatically discovered pruning criteria. For each backbone, all methods use the same eligible tensor scope and requested eligible-weight sparsity. Methods that require calibration use the same calibration budget. No method uses recovery training or fine-tuning after pruning.

We evaluate rollout success under unstructured pruning on LIBERO-Spatial, LIBERO-Object, and LIBERO-Goal. Within each policy backbone, all methods operate on the same predefined eligible parameter scope at the same target eligible-weight sparsity. Unless otherwise stated, we evaluate pruning ratios from 10% to 60% . The Dense row denotes the unpruned policy. Table I summarizes the MATE results. At low and mod-erate sparsity, several baselines can remain competitive on individual suites. For example, Taylor and Pruner-Zero retain competitive performance on LIBERO-Object up to 40% 

pruning. However, no compared baseline exhibits consis-tently robust behavior across all three suites as sparsity increases. Random pruning collapses rapidly, reaching near-zero suc-cess after 20% pruning. Magnitude pruning exhibits abrupt failures on both LIBERO-Spatial and LIBERO-Object, while Wanda and Auto-Prune retain more performance at interme-diate sparsity but also deteriorate sharply in the high-sparsity regime. These results show that weight-only or activation-based criteria can be highly sensitive to the policy suite and target pruning ratio. Gradient-based baselines are more resilient in selected settings, but their robustness is likewise suite-dependent. Tay-lor and Pruner-Zero preserve relatively strong performance on LIBERO-Object at moderate sparsity, but both degrade substantially as sparsity increases. In comparison, LCAM retains 65 .5% , 68 .5% , and 55 .0% success on LIBERO-Spatial, LIBERO-Object, and LIBERO-Goal, respectively, at 60% pruning. This comparison indicates that LCAM provides more consistent high-sparsity retention across the three MATE suites. We additionally observe non-monotonic success-rate curves for both LCAM and the baselines. In closed-loop manipulation, small changes in predicted actions can alter the execution trajectory, contact sequence, or outcome of an episode. Consequently, rollout success need not decrease monotonically with the unstructured pruning ratio, even though sufficiently aggressive pruning eventually degrades policy behavior. CONCLUSIONS We introduce LCAM, a training-free post-training method for unstructured pruning of robotic manipulation policies. LCAM uses loss-conditioned activation evidence to rank connections and applies self-recursive coarse-to-fine prun-ing to construct nested masks from offline demonstrations alone, without recovery training or simulator rollouts. Across three LIBERO suites with MATE policies, LCAM retains substantial task success at high pruning ratios where several compared baselines sharply degrade or collapse. The evalu-ation on OpenVLA further shows that LCAM achieves the highest success among the compared methods at 50% and 

60% pruning; at 50% pruning, it retains over 90% of the dense policy’s success rate. Overall, these results support loss-conditioned, activation-aware importance estimation as a promising training-free direction for preserving robotic policy behavior under high unstructured sparsity. LCAM also has limitations. Offline action distortion is a surrogate for closed-loop task success and may not fully capture long-horizon interaction effects at extreme sparsity. Moreover, the current evaluation covers three MATE suites and one OpenVLA setting, so broader generalization across policy architectures, robot platforms, and real-world tasks remains to be established. Future work may explore offline pruning criteria that better reflect long-horizon action effects and evaluate LCAM across a wider range of robotic policy families and manipulation settings. APPENDIX 

A. Pruning Data Protocol 

For each policy backbone, we distinguish calibration data, held-out allocation data, and final evaluation data. The cali-bration set Dcal is used to estimate LCAM statistics, whereas the disjoint allocation set Dval is used only to compute offline action distortion for automatic knee detection and fine-stage candidate-mask selection. Final evaluation data are reserved exclusively for reporting policy performance and are never accessed during mask construction. MATE uses its native dataset splits, whereas OpenVLA uses the corresponding RLDS-formatted LIBERO data. Within each backbone, all compared methods use the same fixed pruning seed when constructing the calibration and allocation streams. 

B. MATE Pruning Implementation 

For MATE, we apply post-training unstructured weight pruning to all linear layers except those in the frozen track branch, including the action-prediction head. To avoid fully disconnecting individual input or output features, we use the same row- and column-retention safeguards for all compared pruning methods. The native MATE action-prediction objective is the mean-squared error between the predicted continuous actions and the demonstrated actions: 

LMATE act = MSE (ˆ a, a) . (5) This loss is evaluated on calibration batches only to obtain the gradients used by LCAM. LCAM statistics are estimated from 16 calibration batches, whereas the offline action distortion D(M) defined in Eq. (3) is evaluated on 16 batches from the held-out allocation set Dval . Data augmentation is disabled for both streams during pruning. Before mask construction, we cache the dense-policy outputs πθ (o) for o ∈ D val and use them as the common reference for evaluating all candidate masks. The per-dimension standard-deviation vector σ in Eq. (3) is estimated from the calibration stream, and we set the stability floor to τ = 0 .05 . During the second LCAM pass, at most 2048 action-supervised positions are used for each layer call when accumulating joint moments. The automatic controller follows the validation-controlled coarse-to-fine procedure in Section III-B. It uses global sparsity increments of 10% during the coarse stage and 1% during the fine stage. The knee test is activated after two prior coarse marginal distortions are available, with the sensitivity multiplier set to β = 2 .0. LCAM statistics are recomputed for each coarse candidate and at the beginning of every fine allocation round. The controller retains a CPU-side snapshot of the most recently accepted coarse model. If a coarse candidate triggers the knee criterion, it is rejected and the model is restored to the latest accepted snapshot, which defines the boundary for fine allocation. At each fine allocation step, equal-size candidate pruning blocks are formed for all feasible functional groups. These groups correspond to the action-prediction head, spatial-attention modules, spatial MLP modules, temporal Trans-former modules, and the remaining encoder modules. Con-nections within each candidate group are selected using the current LCAM-native criterion under the same retention safeguards. The candidate with the smallest increase in offline action distortion per newly pruned connection is accepted and applied as the next sparsity block. Neither recovery training nor simulator rollouts are used during mask construction. 

C. OpenVLA Pruning Implementation 

For OpenVLA, we use the action-token prediction loss as Lact when computing LCAM scores for the Llama Transformer projection matrices; the visual encoder is left unchanged. LCAM statistics are estimated from 16 calibra-tion samples. Each sample is constructed from one transition selected from an RLDS training episode. For OpenVLA, the native action-prediction objective is the mean negative log-likelihood over the supervised action-token positions: 

LOpenVLA act = − mean t∈T sup log pθ (yt | o, y <t ) , (6) where Tsup denotes the supervised positions in the action-token target sequence. This loss is used only to obtain LCAM gradients during calibration. The selected Llama projection matrices contain approx-imately 6.4 billion weights in the OpenVLA-7B model. Storing both joint-moment accumulators for all target layers simultaneously is therefore impractical. We use a memory-bounded implementation in which the first pass collects the output-gradient RMS for every target layer. During the second pass, Transformer blocks are processed in chunks of two blocks. For a layer call containing more than 2048 token positions, a deterministic subset of 2048 positions is used for moment accumulation. OpenVLA uses the same validation-controlled coarse-to-fine controller as MATE, with global sparsity increments of 10% and 1% in the coarse and fine stages, respectively. The knee test is activated after two preceding coarse marginal distortions have been observed, with sensitivity multiplier 

β = 2 .0.For each block chunk, masks are computed from the same parent checkpoint and stored temporarily as bit-packed files. Model weights remain unchanged until all chunks have com-pleted calibration, after which the newly selected masks are applied together. Thus, all layers within one nested pruning stage are scored against the same parent model. Existing zeros are preserved across successive stages, yielding nested masks.
