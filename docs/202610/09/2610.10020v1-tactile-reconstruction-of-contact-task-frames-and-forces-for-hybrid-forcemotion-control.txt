# Tactile Reconstruction of Contact Task Frames and Forces for Hybrid Force/Motion Control 

Antonio Rapuano 1, Simone Orelli 1, Barbara Bazzana 2, Antonio Franchi 1,2, and Alessandro De Luca 1

Abstract —Hybrid force/motion control requires knowledge of the interaction force and of a task frame defining the force-and motion-controlled directions. These quantities are usually obtained from force/torque sensing or model-based residuals, often assuming also a nominal environment model. This work addresses the online estimation of the contact force and a possibly time-varying task frame using only soft optical tactile sensing, under the assumption of locally planar contact with a negligible contact moment. The proposed method maps a single image of the deformed elastomer of a soft optical tactile sensor to observable contact variables: indentation depth, two surface-to-sensor tilt angles, and 3D contact force, each with a per-sample uncertainty estimate. The mapping is learned through a self-labeling acquisition procedure, in which a manipulator imposes controlled contacts while an auxiliary Force/Torque sensor is used offline to provide ground-truth labels. The tactile measurement is then fused with robot proprioceptive data in an Extended Kalman Filter, producing a continuously updated estimate of the contact task frame and of the interaction force. Control experiments with a DigiTac sensor mounted on a UR10 manipulator demonstrate closed-loop contact force regulation against a flat rigid board in linear and angular motion by a human operator, with touch as the only exteroceptive feedback. 

Index Terms —Force Control, Force and Tactile Sensing, Com-pliance and Impedance Control, Soft Optical Tactile Sensing 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# PHYSICAL interaction with the environment is central to many robotic applications, including manufacturing, inspection, service robotics, and human-robot collaboration. When the task specifies both how the robot should move and how it should exchange forces with the environment, stan-dard control tools include admittance, impedance, and hybrid force/motion control. Admittance control reacts to a measured contact force with a velocity command [1]. Impedance control addresses physical interaction by shaping the dynamic rela-tionship between motion and force [2]. Hybrid force/motion control assigns force along selected task directions and motion along the complementary ones, as formalized in the classical task-frame formulation [3]. These approaches and their rela-tion to manipulator interaction control are reviewed in [4].

Hybrid force/motion control requires, in addition to robot proprioception, knowledge of the interaction force and of a task frame that defines the local force- and motion-controlled directions. In most implementations, this information is obtained from sources other than local tactile deformation. Force/Torque (F/T) sensors provide accurate wrench measurements, but they add dedicated hardware and return a spatially aggregated signal, which is usually displaced from the actual contact. When such sensors are unavailable, external wrenches can be estimated from proprioception through model-based residuals. Residual-based contact and collision estimation is treated systematically in [5]. Such estimates have also been used to implement sensorless collision detection and hy-brid force/motion control [6]. Their integration into hybrid force/velocity control has been demonstrated for physical human-robot collaboration tasks [7]. However, residual meth-ods estimate generalized forces acting on the robot structure. Recovering the local contact point, surface direction, and task frame from these estimates requires additional assumptions and remains sensitive to modeling errors [8]. The task frame is often specified from a geometric model of the environment [9]. When the contact geometry is not known accurately, several approaches instead estimate its relevant directions during interaction. Dynamic hybrid position/force control estimating unknown environmental constraints was achieved in [10]. Force/position control on an unknown planar surface was addressed in [11] by adapting the estimated arXiv:2610.10020v1 [cs.RO] 7 Oct 2026 2

surface orientation using force and robot motion measure-ments. Contact force and the unknown surface gradient can be jointly estimated using the latter to construct the force-and motion-control subspaces [12]. The surface normal can be also estimated from F/T measurements and commanded motion while compensating for friction-induced bias [13]. These approaches demonstrate that the task frame can be recovered online. However, the geometric information is in-ferred through external force measures, proprioception, and closed-loop motion signals that are themselves influenced by the contact mechanics and the resulting interaction. Tactile sensing offers a complementary route because it observes the contact patch rather than only the net wrench or robot motion. Soft skins provide spatially distributed measure-ments of local contact deformation [14]. Vision-based tactile sensors can use this deformation to estimate both contact geometry and force [15]. Soft Optical Tactile (SOT) sensors encode the deformation of a compliant pad into an image. The TacTip family realizes this principle through the motion of internal markers that follow the deformation of its soft surface [16]. The DigiTac sensor adopted in this work com-bines this biomimetic pin arrangement with a compact optical design [17]. The resulting image contains local geometric information about the contact that is not directly available from a spatially aggregated wrench measurement. Recent tactile servoing methods exploit this information by learning contact pose variables from tactile images and regulating them to a reference condition. Pose servoing using a novel soft optical tactile sensor with learned pose estimates has been demonstrated in [18]. Pose-and-shear tactile servoing extends this approach by incorporating shear information into the regulated tactile representation [19]. Learned tactile predic-tions have also been propagated through a Bayesian filter using an associated estimate of uncertainty [20]. However, these tactile servoing approaches typically shape the interaction force implicitly by regulating a tactile pose or deformation condition, rather than assigning a contact force within a hybrid force/motion decomposition. Force regression methods are also available that estimate interaction forces from tactile images. FeelAnyForce targets 3D contact force estimation across vision-based tactile sensors [21]. Learning supervised by finite element analysis has been used to estimate force distributions from a GelSight Mini sensor [22]. Such force estimates do not provide by themselves the local task frame needed to define force- and motion-controlled directions. While each item stands by itself, the missing part is the integration of tactile pose and 

force estimation into the contact variables required by hybrid force/motion control. This work addresses this gap for locally planar, featureless surfaces with an applied contact force but with negligible con-tact moment. Under these assumptions, different positions on the contact plane as well as different tilted directions forming the same angle with the normal to the plane are not observable (indistinguishable) from tactile images. Nevertheless, these im-ages can encode the observable contact geometry, namely the indentation depth and the surface-to-sensor tilt, together with the 3D contact force transmitted through the contact patch. The proposed method learns a mapping from a single SOT image to this tactile measurement, with an associated per-sample un-certainty estimate. The tactile measurement is then fused with robot proprioceptive data to obtain a continuously updated esti-mate of the contact plane and of the corresponding contact task frame. The same estimator tracks the interaction force while contact is active. These estimates are used by an admittance-based hybrid force/motion controller, closing all three transla-tional channels of force regulation from tactile feedback alone. Figure 1 summarizes the resulting architecture. The auxiliary F/T sensor is excluded from the control loop: it is used only offline as ground truth to construct training labels and to provide an independent measurement for experimental comparison. During operation, the system receives only robot proprioceptive data and tactile images, reconstructs the contact state, and supplies the resulting estimates of task frame and interaction force to the controller. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first integrated system that reconstructs both a contact task frame and a 3D interaction force from soft optical tactile images, propagates the learned tactile uncertainty through a state estimator, and uses the reconstructed quantities to close a hybrid force/motion control loop without F/T feedback or an environment model. A hybrid force/motion controller requires, at each time instant, a task frame and knowledge of the interaction force. In the considered setting, these quantities are not assumed from an environment model nor measured by an F/T sensor during the control task. Instead, they are inferred from the image produced by a soft optical tactile sensor. This image encodes the deformation of the elastomer, whose relation to contact geometry and force depends on the pad mechanics, the optical path, and the morphology of the internal pins. An accurate closed-form inverse of this mapping is not available and therefore is extracted from data, and used in real-time control to estimate the observable contact variables. 

Assumption 1 (Planar, featureless contact): The contact surface is locally planar and featureless, its extent is large with respect to the contact patch of the tactile sensor. Its edges do not enter the sensing area. Under Assumption 1, the local task frame associated with the contact plane is determined only up to transformations that leave the tactile image unchanged. Different positions of the sensor over a featureless plane and different tilted directions forming the same angle with the normal to the plane do not modify the local geometry observed by the tactile sensor. Thus, the identifiable parts of the contact are the signed offset d along the surface normal n, together with the two angles that determine the direction of n. Stated differently, among the relative sensor-surface pose variables, touch can resolve the indentation depth and the two tilt angles between the surface normal and the approach direction of the sensor. The tangential position and the twist angle about the normal are structurally unobservable from a single tactile contact with a locally planar, featureless surface [18], [23]. 

Assumption 2 (Negligible contact moment): The moment transmitted at the contact interface is negligible over the intended operating range, because the contact patch is small relative to the characteristic scale of the task. A general contact wrench has six components, three forces and three torques. Under Assumption 2, however, the contact information retained by the measurement model is restricted to the three force components. No contact moment is therefore available for regulation of the torque components. The two assumptions above determine the force/motion decompositions considered in this work. Out of the set of task-space force/motion directions, only the observable ones are subject to control, which discards three components from motion control, namely the two tangential in-plane directions and the angular twist about the normal to the plane. Moreover, due to Assumption 2, the three components of the moment are necessarily set to zero. As a result, the six remaining directions that can be controlled admit the decompositions summarized in Tab. I. The normal and tangential force directions are not equiva-lent from a contact mechanics point of view. Tangential forces can be transmitted only within the friction cone, whose size is determined by the normal load. The same image format is used during training and evaluation, and later for control. IV. T ACTILE MEASUREMENT REGRESSOR 

A tactile image represents a spatially structured deformation field, in which information is encoded by the displacement of the pins. Convolution provides a natural inductive bias for this type of signal and therefore forms the basis of the proposed regressor. 

A. Architecture 

The proposed model (Fig. 4) is a ResNet-style regres-sor [24]. It combines a residual convolutional backbone, which learns a representation of the contact, with two parallel multilayer perceptron (MLP) heads that predict the mean and log-variance of the tactile measurement in R6, respectively. The architecture combines elements whose advantages are established in the literature. Residual connections are known to ease the optimization of deep convolutional backbones [24]. Starting from a residual backbone with four stages, candidate modifications were introduced one at a time and retained when they reduced the validation loss. Examples include group normalization (GN) [25] in place of batch normalization, squeeze-and-excitation (SE) [26], trunk depth and width, size of the pooled feature map, width of the hidden layers, dropout rate, and optimization hyperparameters such as the learning rates. Since each retained change became the baseline for the next, these comparisons are not independent and are therefore not reported as an ablation study. b) Two-Stage Training: Training proceeds in two stages. During homoscedastic pretraining , the variance head is frozen, while a single global log-variance vector shared across all input samples is optimized jointly with the remainder of the network as an auxiliary learnable parameter. Warming up the mean while the variance is held fixed has been shown to pre-vent the convergence failures instead observed when both out-puts are optimized from initialization [28]. The failures arise because the NLL loss suppresses the gradient on poorly fitted samples, yielding a stable yet inaccurate mean predictor [29]. 

Heteroscedastic fine-tuning then starts from the Stage 1 early-stopping checkpoint and optimizes all parameters end-to-end, including the log-variance, using a reduced learning rate. In this stage, the network learns input-dependent, per-sample uncertainty estimates, assigning a larger predicted log-variance in ˆs to ambiguous or unusual tactile images. V. T ACTILE -D RIVEN HYBRID FORCE /M OTION CONTROL 

The control objective is to regulate a prescribed contact force against a surface whose pose is unknown and slowly time-varying, while keeping the alignment of the tactile sensor with the local surface normal. Touch is the only source of exteroceptive feedback available to the controller. The system consists of a fixed-base manipulator equipped with the SOT sensor at its tool tip, using the same mechanical configuration adopted for data acquisition (Fig. 2). The F/T sensor is still present but no longer used for control. In the architecture shown in Fig. 5, an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) [31] fuses each instantaneous tactile measurement with robot proprioceptive data to maintain a continuously updated estimate of contact task frame and interaction force. A target generator issues the hybrid target, composed by the desired contact force and the desired sensor orientation. An admittance layer combines these targets with the estimated contact task frame and converts them into smooth motion references, which are tracked by a geometric Cartesian velocity controller.

The tactile measurement returned by the regressor is fused with robot proprioceptive data in a contact state estimator based on EKF, producing a continuously updated estimate of contact task frame and interaction force used in an admittance-based hybrid force/motion controller. Control experiments have demonstrated contact force regulation against a flat, rigid board undergoing linear and angular motion imposed by a human operator. Within the tested operating conditions, the system was able to keep the prescribed contact force while adapting the sensor orientation to the estimated surface normal, using touch provided by the SOT sensor as the only exteroceptive feedback in the control loop. The scope of the obtained results is defined by the contact model and acquisition domain considered in this work. The dataset covers rigid, planar, featureless surfaces under moderate indentation and within the prescribed contact cone. Thus, we do not claim a priori generalization to larger shear-to-normal force ratios or to curved, textured, or compliant sur-faces. Extending acquisition and measurement models to such contact conditions is an important direction of future work. The decomposition in Tab. I also identifies directions that remain available for extending the control formulation. Rotation about the surface normal is unobservable under the adopted contact model and is currently fixed through an arbitrary task augmentation. This remaining degree of freedom could instead be assigned to a secondary objective, such as avoidance of kinematic singularities or joint limits of the manipulator. Furthermore, although tangential contact position is structurally unobservable on a featureless plane, tangential motion may leave observable signatures through shear and incipient slip [32]. A tactile velocity observer could therefore support motion control along tangential directions when the desired interaction cannot be realized within the friction cone. We remark that the considered contact task, and in particular the reconstructed contact variables, is not specific to fixed-base manipulators. Its integration on a floating-base robotic system (such as an aerial platform) would require fusion with the corresponding onboard state estimator and an interaction controller accounting for coupled base and robot dynamics. Investigating this extension is particularly relevant to operations in which physical contact is essential and dedicated environment or contact wrench sensing through a F/T sensor is unavailable. Finally, beyond force/motion regulation, the same SOT-based estimate of the contact geometry may constitute an additional local exteroceptive measurement that could be fused into the platform localization and mapping pipeline, complementing vision- or lidar-based estimation in visually degraded or feature-poor environments.
