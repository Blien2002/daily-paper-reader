# Distributed Cascade Force Control of Soft-Tactile-Based Multi-robot System for Object Transportation 

Duy Anh Nguyen 1, Nhat Minh Dinh Le 2, Nhan Huu Nguyen 2, Pham Duy Hung 3,Van Anh Ho 2,4 and Trung Dung Ngo 1

Abstract — In this paper, we present a distributed cascade force control system (DCFC) for multiple robots with the aim of pushing a rigid object towards a desired moving target without their inter-robot communication. These mobile robots are equipped with 360-degree vision-based soft tactile sensors utilized to determine contact location and resultant impact force. By investigating the dynamics of moving rigid objects on the flat, we proposed a distributed cascade control. The inner loop control incorporates contact force and positioning, ensuring the robots’ pushing contact and application of the desired force to the object. The outer loop control coordinates the robots to push the object in a desired direction without inter-robot communication, regardless of the unknown object mass. The stability and convergence of the system are verified using the Lyapunov stability theory. We also conducted simulation and real-word experiments to validate the performance of the proposed control method, and the experimental results showcase the successful coordination of multiple robots in pushing an object towards a moving desired direction. 

I. INTRODUCTION Collective object transportation by multiple robots can be employed in many practical applications, such as smart warehouses, construction sites, and search and rescue mis-sions. To achieve collaborative tasks, a distributed control system for consensus decision-making among the robots conducted at the individual level based on local information and interactions with neighbouring robots is required. The advantages of a distributed control system for multi-robot systems are known as scalability, robustness, and adaptability when deployed in dynamic and uncertain environments. This allows the robotic system to handle the complexity of a given task and the number of robots required for the task, such as heavy object transportation, inspired by the ants as illustrated in Fig. 1. In general, pushing is more challenging than grasping because no additional gripping tool is needed while the dynamics of rigid objects are critically required. The primitive strategy for multiple mobile robots to trans-port an object involves controlling robots’ velocity and/or formation while dealing with forces between the object, robots, and the surface as disturbances. Inspired by collective behaviours in social insects in [1], the authors proposed simple group behaviour-based algorithms for pushing a box forward without communication. These algorithms were ex-tended by employing sensors to detect the goal, allowing the robots to relocate around and push the object when the goal was detected [2]. Further studies analyzed the stagnation recovery based on ants’ behavior and tested the box-pushing algorithm by tracking changing goals with different types of boxes [3], [4]. However, these kinetic strategies have limitations as they did not account for the physical interaction between the object and the surface, which plays a critical role in object transportation, especially across various terrains or slopes [5]. Uncontrollable forces between the object and the robots can exceed safety limits and harm both the robots and the object, particularly fragile ones. These methods may lead to inaccuracy in task performance as they do not incorporate rigid-body object dynamics to represent object behaviour. Recent studies addressed the above-mentioned problems by considering object dynamics and robot forces . Grasping techniques with controllable exerted force provided greater control freedom for robots to push or pull objects with the motion of local objects measured by each robot as implicit information for force coordination [6]–[8]. In [6], it was proved that all the robots’ contact forces converged to a leader force direction, resulting in a force-amplifying effect. An extension of this work considered both force and torque consensus [7], as well as steering the leader’s force to navigate the system in narrow spaces [8]. In other directions, decentralized adaptive control leveraged the theory of adaptive control method to estimate system parameters and manipulate the object simultaneously [9], [10]. The desired object states, such as linear and angular velocity and desired trajectories, were achieved and proved through the Lyapunov stability theory. Within the scope of pushing methods, some researchers proposed collective transportation strategies based on occlusion, where robots only pushed the object when their vision of the goal was obscured [11], [12]. C. Interaction force between robot and object surface 

When a robot touches or pushes an object, there exists an interactive force between the robot and the object’s surface. This force includes both the perpendicular force to the object’s surface and the frictional force between them. It is important to note that this friction prevents the sliding between the tactile sensor and the object’s surface. Furthermore, if there is minimal relative motion between the robot and the object (i.e., vi ≈ vG), especially in the steady state when ω ≈ 0, the friction between the two surfaces can be ignored. We conducted a Matlab simulation to model the behaviour of robots and objects based on the rigid object dynamics equations, differential drive robot models, and our proposed control DCFC. The simulation results are depicted in Fig. 5 and the stability and convergence of both the inner and outer loop control objectives were validated. Regarding the inner loop control, the measured force consistently converged towards the desired force signal, as shown in Fig. 5a, represented by Eq. 13. In Fig. 5b, the contact angles θi of both robots also converge to zero as in Eq. 14. As shown in Fig. 5c, the object’s motion direction is closely aligned with the desired direction, meaning the angle error converges to zero. Meanwhile, the object’s rotational behavior remains stable as its angular velocity approaches zero. Note that the settling time of the entire system is approximately 6 seconds.

C. Discussion 

Our simulation and real-world experiments have validated the reasonable convergence time and stability of our pro-posed DCFC control system. Certain system parameters, par-ticularly those related to friction, are subject to uncertainty because the surface conditions in real-world experiments do not remain as constant as they are in the model. The hardware results collectively confirm that our proposed DCFC control scheme successfully overcame these uncertainties, enabling robots to transport the object to the moving target. Although the control policy can handle unknown object mass and friction parameters, f0 and the number of robots are pre-defined to counteract the static friction between the object and the surface. The exact number of robots and their positions are not critical as long as there is sufficient space on the object’s boundary for them. This setup can be exemplified by positioning two robots on the left and right edges of the object. In the near future, we aim to deploy more robots to transport objects with indeterminate masses and to handle asymmetric pushing positions, thereby assessing the robustness of the designed control system. Moreover, we plan to introduce a pre-pushing phase where robots determine suitable pushing positions using their local sensors for more effective object manipulation. The control system currently does not account for the specific shape of the object, provided that the shape allows the distribution of the robots on two sides of the center of mass along a straight edge, such as in triangles or certain polygons. In subsequent developments, we will enhance the algorithm to cater to a wider range of object shapes, especially those with non-polygonal geometries and non-uniform mass distributions. VI. CONCLUSIONS In conclusion, our proposed distributed cascade force control system (DCFC) helps soft-tactile-based robots push an unknown object in a desired direction without commu-nication. Moreover, robots can successfully exert desired force and maintain pushing contact while transporting ob-jects using deformable tactile sensors. The two-loop cascade control system demonstrated coordination capacity among robot groups, enabling precise object transportation with proven convergence and stability theory. The insights gained from this research will advance the field of cooperative object transportation with soft-tactile-based robots, leading to more robust and versatile robotic systems for real-world applications.
