# Nudge Before You Push: Physics-Aware Navigation via Tactile Probing

Abstract: Visually identical containers can conceal loads that require different handling decisions. We present TAN AV , which uses a brief nudge to measure push resistance for navigation under a site-defined handling boundary. T AC PHYS reads the force sequence, with optional RGB-D and kinematics, into a mass estimate for push authorization. A repeated-patrol planner weighs probe and route costs, requests a second contact when useful, and reuses observations across visits. In simulation, T AC PHYS approaches a resistance-only Bayes reference and reduces missed pushes from 28.7% to 5.5% relative to peak-force thresholding at compa-rable low-risk operating points. In repeated-patrol simulation, TAN AV recovers 90% of the oracle’s path saving, more than halves human interventions relative to always-detour, and reduces boundary violations from 4.3% to 2.9% relative to RGB-D-only probing. On a quadruped manipulator with a Hall-array fingertip, offline zero-shot MAE is 0.28–1.07 kg on containers up to 2.82 kg. Force-rise cali-bration at 3 kg gives 93.5% pooled offline accuracy (86.4% on non-cube episodes); a separate raw-peak rule gives 15 of 20 correct online decisions on unseen boxes. 

Keywords: Interactive Navigation, Tactile Sensing, Physical Property Estimation 

## 1 Introduction 

Industrial patrol robots revisit routes that may change between inspection cycles [1, 2]. We consider staging areas where temporarily placed cartons or totes can obstruct familiar paths. Box-like contain-ers can conceal different payloads behind similar exteriors, while accessible, approximately planar sides allow repeatable probing from complementary directions. Before moving an unfamiliar box, a person nudges it to feel resistance. TAN AV turns this intuition into a mass estimate that informs the route-clearing decision. The decision also differs from mechanical movability. Navigation among movable obstacles (NAMO) uses movability or manipulation cost inferred from attempts or appearance [3, 4]. A site instead permits clearing light containers and reserves heavier loads for operators, even if both are movable. The decision thus compares mass with a site-defined boundary auditable against known loads. TAN AV separates contact interpretation, push authorization, and route choice (Fig. 1). A gripper-mounted fingertip records nudge resistance; T AC PHYS reads force sequences into mass for comparison with the site boundary. The planner checks eligibility before comparing push and detour costs, requests useful additional contacts, and reuses observations across visits to amortize probing. Hardware tests assess offline mass transfer and calibrated force decisions; patrol evaluation is simulated. The mass estimate also parameterizes simulated patrol cost. Auxiliary center-of-mass (CoM) classes describe load distribution and support analysis of the information provided by the probe. The nudge supplies evidence for authorization. The tested try-push and appearance-prior policies reach similar autonomy but authorize 55.5% of heavy containers in simulated patrol. RGB-D obser-vations during the nudge leave 4.3% violations, force sequences 2.9%, and two-contact agreement 1.0%. Caching observations amortizes contact cost across visits. ## 2 Related Work 

Navigation among movable obstacles. NAMO extends geometric planning by allowing a robot to rearrange obstacles [3], including under incomplete knowledge of movability [5, 4]. Subsequent methods infer affordances, manipulation cost, or movability from observations [6, 7, 8]. Mobile manipulators also combine navigation with learned pushing control and interaction-state estimation [9, 10]; tactile skins support negotiation of obstacles during contact [11]. We study a diagnostic contact before route clearing, using its response to evaluate an authorization boundary and comparing readers under the same planner in simulation. 2Physical inference through contact. Interactive perception uses actions to reveal properties that passive sensing cannot resolve [12]. Inertial estimation can use manipulator excitation [13], tactile in-hand interaction [14], or non-prehensile pushing [15, 16]. Pushing mechanics and experimental datasets show how friction and contact geometry affect the response [17, 18, 19]. Optical tactile sensors provide deformation measurements [20, 21], with calibrated force regressors supporting push-based weighing [22]; magnetic arrays provide local loading measurements [23, 24]. We use a force sequence as the simulation–hardware interface and assess the information needed for a navigation decision. 

## 3 Method 

3.1 Decision and Probe 

A box-like container with an accessible side face and unknown mass m blocks the patrol route. We consider containers for which the site permits the prescribed diagnostic nudge. A site-defined handling boundary τ governs the subsequent route-clearing push: objects with m ≤ τ are eligible, while heavier objects must be bypassed or referred to an operator. We evaluate 3 kg as the main boundary. A reader supplies an eligibility judgment, with optional deferral. For a learned mass estimate ˆm,

In simulation, the planner also checks whether an eligible clearing push reduces route cost. The robot’s physical push limit separately determines whether an attempted push succeeds; we vary it independently of τ in the patrol evaluation. The probe precedes the route-clearing push. On hardware, the arm approaches at 3 cm/s until contact, rests for 0.3 s, advances 4–7 cm at a speed sampled log-uniformly from 0.10–0.25 m/s, dwells for 0.3 s, and retracts. Contact position varies; median duration, including approach and retraction, is 2.6 s. The controller stops commanded motion near joint limits; aborted probes permit operator-commanded retraction and restoration of joystick control. A second nudge can contact an orthogonal face for another view of the response. In simulation, the robot repositions to the adjacent face. The main simulation protocol begins contact at a sampled speed of 0.15–0.35 m/s. Additional datasets use a hardware-like creep, rest, and speed ramp to test dependence on contact onset. Same-face repetitions control for the benefit of changing geometry. 

3.2 Learned Sequence Reader: TacPhys 

Nudge k provides force F k, kinematics qk, and an RGB-D clip Ik. We align the streams to push onset on a 60 Hz grid, retaining eight pre-onset and 96 post-onset samples; missing samples are padded and masked. Force has three components. The 37-dimensional kinematic vector contains end-effector pose and velocity, joint positions and velocities, motor torques, and position targets. Eight RGB-D frames span the push, each resized to 224 × 224 .MLPs tokenize force and kinematics; a ResNet-style encoder projects RGB-D frames to the same width of 192. Time, modality, and nudge embeddings identify each token, and a rotation embedding describes the second contact. A six-layer, six-head transformer [25] processes the combined sequence. Its [CLS] feature feeds a log-mass head and three auxiliary center-of-mass (CoM) classifiers. Each CoM class denotes the low, middle, or high third of an object axis in the first-contact frame. Only mass enters the navigation decision. The temporal encoder has 2.67M parameters; the full model has 14.2M. 3.3 Site Calibration and Agreement 

Two readings implement the site boundary. TAC PHYS supplies continuous mass estimates for simulated patrol cost and reuse when τ changes. A one-parameter force-rise boundary fitted on known loads is auditable with a scale and supports authorization beyond the fingertip’s mass-resolution range, near 4 kg here. An affine map corrects learned mass on the same loads; both calibrations are fitted once per site and fingertip. Both domains provide force in newtons. We evaluate an optical DIGIT fingertip with a learned force regressor [22] and a 120-taxel Hall array (MagPad), resampled from 36 Hz to the model’s 60 Hz grid. The array sum is scale-calibrated at 20.7 counts/N (R2 = 0 .94 ). Hall-array mass evaluations subtract each nudge’s mean force vector over valid samples in the eight-sample pre-onset window. ## 4 Experiments 

4.1 Data and Evaluation Simulation. We collect 51,200 paired episodes in Isaac Lab [26] using a B1+Z1 digital twin and procedural cuboids; 48,731 pass checks for complete contacts, valid observations, and stable resets. Mass is sampled log-uniformly over [0 .5, 15] kg, independently of size, appearance, and object and floor friction. Edge ranges are 0.3–0.5, 0.3–0.6, and 0.3–0.9 m. Static and dynamic friction ranges are 0.3–0.9 and 0.2–0.8, with dynamic friction capped by static friction and arithmetic averaging at contact. Contact location, speed, CoM, and lighting vary. Second contacts are orthogonal with probability 0.75 and otherwise use the same face. Train/validation/test splits are 80/10/10 by collection session, with both contacts kept together. 

Hardware. A Unitree B1 with a Z1 arm carries the fingertip and an Azure Kinect (Fig. 2). The Hall-array set contains 262 clean paired episodes: 196 on a 1.69 kg cube with 15 CoM configurations, and 66 on 11 mass instances of six containers spanning 1.76–10.52 kg. These are five cardboard boxes at two loads each and a square plastic waste container, recorded on carpet and a plastic sheet. Of the 262 pairs, 220 use an orthogonal second face and 42 repeat on the same face as controls. Archived zero-shot and affine mass estimates use the 220 orthogonal pairs, matching the evaluated model’s training protocol. The calibrated boundary and agreement rules apply to both protocols and use all 262 pairs. The optical set contains 554 single nudges on 13 objects spanning 0.74–12.96 kg and a calibration cube, with 480 retained. Optical single nudges support the force-readout comparison (Fig. 4); Hall-array data also support calibration and online evaluation. Each front end’s force–mass response is characterized on its own collection. 

Metrics and splits. We report mass MAE and accuracy at τ = 3 kg. Decision risk is the fraction of heavy objects authorized to be pushed; missed pushes are the fraction of eligible objects classified for detour. Selective accuracy and risk use accepted episodes, with coverage reported separately. Simulation decision comparisons share 3,618 orthogonal test episodes. Mass metrics use each configuration’s eligible test cohort. Hardware folds hold out mass instances or containers; instance folds may retain another load of the same container.

Navigation benefit. One-nudge TAN AV recovers 90% of the oracle’s path saving (6.1% versus 6.8%) and raises the fraction of autonomous patrol cycles, defined as cycles completed without human intervention, from 78.1% to 89.3% relative to always-detour, with interventions falling from 0.26 to 0.12 per patrol (Table 5). Tactile input primarily reduces risk relative to RGB-D-only probing: path savings are similar, while violations fall from 4.3% to 2.9% and failed pushes from 0.39 to 0.33 per scene. The paired path-saving difference is 0.32 percentage points (95% bootstrap interval over 300 layouts: [0.16, 0.50]); cumulative time over ten cycles, including probes and intervention waits, is 10 1569 versus 1595 s. Try-push reaches 0.11 interventions but authorizes 55.5% of heavy objects. The one-nudge peak policy gives 5.4% saving and 10.3% violations, with 0.96 failed pushes per scene. 

Additional contact. Adaptive second contacts target estimates near the boundary: they retain 6.1% path saving while reducing failed pushes from 0.33 to 0.26 per scene, using 6.6 probes versus 10.2 for fixed two-contact probing. Two fixed nudges reduce violations to 2.4%. Per-contact agreement further reduces them to 1.0 ± 0.2% , with 0.11 ± 0.03 failed pushes per scene, at a cost of 0.2 percentage points in path saving. Peak-force agreement yields 4.4% violations and 0.42 failed pushes. Learned agreement also misses fewer useful pushes, 27.2% versus 37.2%. The τ = 2 –8 kg sweep (Fig. 5c) shows the resulting trade-off: one-nudge TAN AV stays within one percentage point of oracle path saving, with higher violation rates at larger boundaries. A direct classifier with the same encoder, trained to predict m > 3 kg and feeding its probability to the cost rule, achieves similar performance there (5.9% path saving, 2.0% violations), but applying it unchanged at 2 kg gives 10.3% violations versus 3.2% for regression. At 4–8 kg its saving stays near 6%, versus 7–9% for regression; this tests reuse of a fixed-boundary classifier, not retraining for each boundary. 

Observation reuse. Re-probing every encounter leaves mean path saving and violations unchanged at the reported precision, but increases probes from 5.86 to 8.50 per scene and reduces time saving by 1.04 percentage points. In a separate 300-layout sensitivity set, missing 25/50/100% of replacements raises violations from 3.3% to 5.8/9.0/16.9%; re-probing stays at 3.3%, motivating reassociation checks or cache invalidation. 

## 5 Discussion 

In simulation, learned mass supports both push authorization and patrol cost. Site calibration relates resistance to the handling boundary under local contact conditions; mass and friction remain coupled. A change in τ therefore calls for calibration around the new boundary, as the 2 kg evaluation shows. 

## 6 Limitations 

Hardware calibration covers seven containers on two floors, with most episodes collected on the cube. Beyond the fingertip’s range, mass estimates compress: with containers held out, the affine reader authorizes 20/39 heavy episodes versus 8/39 for the calibrated boundary. A wider-range fingertip is needed to extend mass estimation to the site’s heavier loads. Real-cube CoM accuracy is 25–40% per axis under quasi-static probing; impulsive onset is the protocol change to test. Further hardware evaluation will address autonomous contact placement, learned-reader decisions online, and object reassociation across patrol visits. 

## 7 Conclusion 

TAN AV connects a diagnostic nudge to navigation through push resistance, mass estimation, site-defined authorization, and route cost. In simulation, T AC PHYS approaches a resistance-only Bayes reference and reduces missed pushes from 28.7% to 5.5%; repeated patrol recovers 90% of the oracle’s path saving and raises autonomous cycles from 78.1% to 89.3%.
