Abstract —Tactile sim-to-real learning must bridge simulated contact and device-specific sensor responses while preserving information needed for control. We propose a factorized tactile representation and control framework that maps normal force and contact patch to an effective contact response recoverable from sensor readings. The response is separated into contact geometry, force distribution, and temporal contact change, with representation-specific encoding and randomization. A Tactile Gated Policy preserves these representations separately through control and operates over all mask configurations without retraining. We evaluate the approach through response reconstruction, spatial alignment, force regulation, and contact-rich adversarial peg insertion in simulation and the real world, enabling the utility and transfer reliability of different tactile representations to be assessed independently. The approach achieves <1 mm contact localization, 1.69 N force-tracking error on unseen geometries, and a 35% improvement in real-world adversarial peg insertion over the unfactorized response, with different tactile representations benefiting different interactions.

For tactile manipulation, however, transferring a policy requires transferring the contact feedback on which its behavior depends. Physical tactile measurements reflect contact mechanics and sensor-specific responses that are difficult to reproduce in simulation. Enabling simulation-trained policies to use this feedback reliably is therefore an important step toward scalable contact-rich manipulation. Recent advances such as TacSL and HydroShear demon-strate the potential of tactile simulation for learning transfer-able contact-rich behaviors [2], [3]. An important remaining challenge is making the information available from compact capacitive arrays equally useful for sim-to-real control. These arrays provide sparse measurements, yet their distributed responses can carry fine-grained information about contact geometry, force distribution, and temporal evolution. Simpli-fying touch into binary contact or compact force-and-location summaries can facilitate transfer, but reduces the information exposed to the controller [4], [5]. We instead seek to retain distributed contact information in a common representation across simulation and hardware, and to use that information in a factorized manner throughout sensing and control. This distinction matters because the information needed for a behavior and the uncertainty it should tolerate are not the same for every component of touch: geometric inference should tolerate variation in response magnitude, force regulation requires loading magnitude to remain informative, and relative temporal changes can remain useful despite uncertainty in absolute sensor gain. Our objective is therefore not only to transfer a richer tactile response, but also to keep different arXiv:2610.10510v1 [cs.RO] 7 Oct 2026 forms of tactile information separately accessible to the policy rather than commit to one representation for every interaction. We introduce factorized tactile representation and control for capacitive tactile arrays (Fig. 1). Normal force and contact patch define an effective contact response r, while per-taxel calibration recovers the same response from sensor readings. From this common response, we construct three tactile representations: contact geometry (G), force distribution (F), and temporal contact change (C). To preserve this factorization through control, we introduce the Tactile Gated Policy. It encodes the three representations separately and supports representation-specific randomization. A tactile mask keeps their latent features independently selectable before they enter the policy. For each task, one policy is trained across all eight mask configurations, allowing us to evaluate each representation and change the active representations at deployment without retraining. Using a parallel gripper equipped with two 28-taxel capacitive pads, we validate the approach from held-out response reconstruction to real manipulation. The representation supports sub-millimeter contact localization with 6.25 × 5.71 mm taxel spacing, while the best force-aware mask achieves 1.69 N real-world force-tracking MAE on unseen geometries. Tactile alignment, grasp-force regulation, and adversarial peg insertion further demonstrate how this common response supports precise and contact-rich control through separately and jointly usable tactile information. Our contributions are: 1) An effective contact response defined from normal force and contact patch and recoverable from capacitive sensor readings, providing a shared sim-to-real tactile interface while retaining distributed spatial and loading information. 2) Factorized tactile representation and control that pre-serve contact geometry, force distribution, and temporal contact change through representation-specific encoding and randomization, while the Tactile Gated Policy keeps them independently selectable within one unified policy. 3) A real-world evaluation on a parallel gripper with two sparse capacitive pads, demonstrating response recon-struction on unseen contact geometries, sub-mm contact localization, grasp-force regulation, and challenging adversarial peg insertion. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Tactile Simulation and Sim-to-Real Transfer 

Tactile simulation has advanced rapidly for vision-based sensors, where the measurement is an image that can be rendered directly. TACTO [6] and Taxim [7] model GelSight-style sensors, while TacSL [2] and Tac2Real [8] bring visuotactile simulation to GPU-parallel policy learning. Taxel arrays require a different mapping from simulated contact to distributed sensor responses. Kasolowsky and B¨ auml [9] model a soft tactile skin by reconstructing local penetration from contact geometry and force, allowing contact to spread across multiple taxels in a rigid-body simulator. A recent digital twin of the TSF-85 instead simulates a deformable sensing field and learns a mapping to physical tactile counts [10]. Our approach avoids reconstructing a deformation field. We define an effective contact response from normal force and contact patch that is recoverable from sensor readings and retains distributed information for policy learning. 

B. Tactile Representations for Sim-to-Real Manipulation 

Tactile representation is itself an important design choice for sim-to-real manipulation. TactileRL shows that abstractions of simulated tactile observations can transfer better than full tactile images [4]. Beyond Binary introduces a physics-grounded center-of-pressure representation that retains force and contact-location information while supporting zero-shot transfer [5]. These works show that successful transfer need not require reproducing the full sensor observation. A parallel line learns transferable tactile embeddings across sensors and modalities, including AnyTouch [11] and TacX [12]. Our objective is different. Rather than compressing touch into one transferable representation, we preserve contact geometry, force distribution, and temporal contact change separately through encoding and control. This allows their utility and sim-to-real behavior to be evaluated independently and their availability to the policy to be changed without retraining. III. F ACTORIZED TACTILE INTERFACE 

Our tactile interface connects simulated contact to sensor readings through a common effective contact response. We first define the contact quantities used to construct this response, then factorize it into geometry, force, and temporal contact information. 

A. Shared Contact State 

We describe contact using two physically interpretable quantities: total normal force F and contact patch A. In simulation, both are obtained from the contact solver. For real-world response identification, they are measured from controlled contacts. This abstraction is appropriate for the relatively stiff tactile pads studied here. Gripper-position measurements indicate less than 1 mm of indentation at grasp forces up to approximately 60 N. We assume uniform pressure over the patch, P = F/ |A| .

C. Response Identification and Operating Range 

We identify the response model from controlled real-world contacts with known F and A and measured cj . A single set of shared parameters (σ, η, γ ) is used for both pads and all taxels. For each candidate (σ, η, γ ), Equation (1) computes 

rj , and the optimal βj is solved in closed form from cj . We select (σ, η, γ ) by reconstruction error across both pads and retain the corresponding βj for each taxel. The identified values are σ = (1 .5, 1.25) mm , η = 0 .7, and γ = 0 .5.The same shared response model is used for both pads throughout the paper. Each pad’s taxel-specific sensitivity is captured only through βj . Section V-B verifies that this shared model reconstructs held-out contacts on both pads and generalizes to unseen contact geometries. The calibrated mapping between rj and cj remains approximately linear up to 20 N per taxel. Above this range, cj compresses relative to the model prediction. We therefore identify and evaluate the tactile interface within this operating range.

To preserve the tactile factorization through control, we introduce the Tactile Gated Policy (Fig. 3). Each representation is encoded separately and selected by a tactile mask before entering the recurrent policy. 

A. Representation-Specific Encoders 

All three representations are encoded into 64-D latents (Fig. 3). The geometry encoder EG hierarchically processes the taxel point set following PointNet++ [13]. Two set-abstraction stages reduce the 56 taxels to 32 and then 8 points using farthest-point sampling and local neighborhoods. Within each neighborhood, learned features are summarized by both max pooling and a response-weighted mean. A final global aggregation uses the same pooling operations, followed by an MLP that produces zG ∈ R64 . The force and change encoders, EF and EC , operate on the two per-pad grids. Each grid first passes through a pad-specific affine adapter and then a CNN shared across the two pads. A fusion MLP combines the two pad features into zF , zC ∈ R64 . EF and EC use the same architecture with separate weights. Fig. 3: Tactile Gated Policy. Separate encoders produce geometry, force, and temporal-change latents. The mask m gates these latents before concatenation with the encoded robot state and mask itself as input to the LSTM actor. 

B. Tactile Mask 

A three-bit tactile mask m = [ mG, m F , m C ] ∈ { 0, 1}3

selects which representation latents are available to the policy. The selected latents are concatenated with the encoded robot state and m, then passed to an LSTM actor. The mask is fixed within each episode, and all eight configurations are sampled during training. The all-zero mask uses proprioception only. One policy is trained per task across all eight configurations, allowing the contribution of each tactile representation to be evaluated and the active representations to be changed at deployment without retraining. 

C. Representation-Specific Randomization 

Factorization also allows tactile uncertainty to be ran-domized selectively during policy training. We perturb each representation along dimensions that preserve its intended information. For G, the normal force is scaled independently on each pad by λs ∼ U (0 .7, 1.3) once per episode, while the contact patch remains unchanged. This perturbs response magnitude while preserving spatial contact structure. For F, the normal force is preserved while the contact patch is rigidly misregistered in the pad plane. Each pad receives an independent rotation and translation sampled once per episode. The transformed patch is used to recompute the pressure field with the original force. This perturbs the spatial distribution while preserving absolute loading. Task-specific ranges are given in Section V-D. C receives no episode-level tactile randomization. Its normalized temporal contrast is already invariant to the static per-taxel gain βj , as shown in Equation (3). Sensor noise is applied upstream of all three representations at every policy step. On hardware, however, GFC achieves the highest observed success at 19/30 (63.3%), compared with 14/30 (46.7%) for Response r, 9/30 (30.0%) for Binary, and 5/30 (16.7%) for proprioception alone. The representation favored in simulation is not the one that performs best after transfer. The factorization ablation shows a consistent pattern. Removing any one representation from GFC reduces hardware success: GF reaches 12/30, while GC and FC each reach 14/30. C is weak alone (8/30), but adding it does not reduce success in any of the four matched comparisons and improves three, consistent with temporal change providing complementary information during transfer. Jointly applying all randomizations to an unfactorized representation does not recover the GFC result: r remains at 14/30 with DR, while CoP changes from 2/30 to 1/30. CoP reaches 64.3% success in simulation but only 6.7% on hardware, below proprioception-only. Simulated collisions produce abrupt lateral centroid shifts that are absent on hardware, suggesting that CoP exposes contact transients that transfer poorly. Proprioception-only still retains indirect contact cues through OSC tracking deviations, which appears to be more reliable in this setting. GFC instead preserves distributed spatial and loading patterns across the pads, providing complementary cues beyond a single magnitude and centroid. Its higher hardware success (19/30) supports retaining richer contact information over a compact summary that may be sensitive to collision-response mismatch. Together, the three tasks show that tactile utility depends on the interaction: G-containing masks support precise alignment, F-containing masks support accurate force regulation, and the complete representation achieves the highest success in integrated insertion. Full tactile input is therefore not uniformly optimal across tasks. Preserving the factorization through the policy keeps these different forms of tactile information independently accessible without retraining. VI. D ISCUSSION 

The effective response r is a phenomenological quantity, not a measurement of the pad’s deformation, pressure, stress, strain, or mechanical state. Its justification is operational: over the declared range it reconstructs held-out physical measurements and preserves usable information, certified only below approximately 20 N per taxel. The property we exploit is recoverability from the real sensor together with the separation of the three kinds of information, not fidelity of appearance. A more faithful forward model could replace the compact response without disturbing anything downstream, which consumes r rather than the model that produced it. Because r follows from a net force and a contact patch rather than a deformable pad state, it is compatible with GPU-parallel policy training. More broadly, tactile information with different transfer properties need not be collapsed into one policy representation: keeping it separately addressable is what turns a representation choice into a deployment option.
