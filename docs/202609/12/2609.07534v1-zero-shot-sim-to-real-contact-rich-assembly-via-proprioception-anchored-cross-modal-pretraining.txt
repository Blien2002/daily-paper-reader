# Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Contact-Rich Assembly via Proprioception-Anchored Cross-Modal Pretraining 

Yuhan Wang 1, Yurou Chen 2, Hongye Jiang 1, and Wenzhao Lian 1,* 

Fig. 1: Overview of the PACE pipeline. A privileged-state expert collects simulated demonstrations containing images, force/torque (F/T) measurements, proprioception, and privileged states. The multimodal encoder is pretrained through proprioceptive anchoring and auxiliary objectives. An asymmetric actor–critic policy is then trained on frozen encoder features and deployed directly on a real robot. 

Abstract — Contact-rich assembly remains challenging be-cause it requires submillimeter spatial accuracy and reliable interpretation of forces during sustained contact. Although simulation-based reinforcement learning offers a scalable train-ing paradigm, discrepancies in visual observations, contact dynamics, and force/torque (F/T) measurements often limit policy transfer. We observe that proprioception is comparatively consistent across domains because calibrated joint positions and consistently computed joint velocities align closely between simulation and hardware. Based on this observation, we present PACE (Proprioception-Anchored Cross-Modal Encoder), which supervises temporal visual and F/T representations by pre-dicting proprioceptive state transitions. Static domain-specific factors, including lighting, texture, and sensor bias, contain little information about joint motion; the proposed objective therefore encourages the encoder to suppress these factors while retaining task-relevant motion cues. Policies trained on frozen PACE features are deployed on hardware without real-world fine-tuning or object-pose tracking. Across four contact-rich assembly tasks, PACE attains an average real-world success rate of 93.3% and only a 2.7-percentage-point sim-to-real drop, meanwhile remaining robust to perturbations that substantially degrade pose-based and learned-fusion baselines.

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Contact-rich assembly remains a fundamental challenge in robot learning. Unlike pick-and-place or tabletop re-arrangement, assembly often involves submillimeter clear-ances, so even small misalignments can cause jamming or failure. Vision alone is therefore insufficient in many settings: successful compliant alignment requires both fine-grained spatial information and accurate interpretation of contact forces. Simulation-based reinforcement learning (RL) provides a scalable approach to acquiring these skills. In a common paradigm, a privileged-state policy is trained in simulation, where ground-truth poses and contact states are available, and is then deployed using object poses estimated by an external perception module [1], [2]. This decoupled design creates a brittle perception–control interface. For assemblies with submillimeter clearances, millimeter-scale pose errors caused by lighting variation, or calibration drift can lead to control errors from which the policy cannot recover. arXiv:2609.07534v1 [cs.RO] 7 Sep 2026 Existing approaches address this gap in three principal ways. Domain randomization [3], [4] varies visual and phys-ical parameters during training, but residual discrepancies may remain large relative to assembly tolerances. Domain adaptation aligns feature distributions across domains [5], [6], but requires real-robot data. System identification [7] calibrates simulation parameters against hardware and is often costly and task-specific. More generally, rendered im-ages, simulated contact dynamics, and synthetic force/torque (F/T) measurements differ systematically from their real-world counterparts. Policies that depend on these signals may therefore fail on hardware even when the commanded robot trajectories are closely matched across domains. To address domain discrepancies in visual and F/T signals, we train an encoder to produce representations that are less sensitive to domain-specific nuisance factors. Our key observation is that proprioceptive measurements are substan-tially more consistent across simulation and hardware than visual or F/T measurements. By requiring temporal visual and F/T features to predict proprioceptive state transitions, the training objective encourages the encoder to suppress static, domain-specific information and preserve the physical motion associated with those transitions. Proprioception thus serves as a cross-domain anchor : it provides a supervisory signal that is available in both simulation and hardware, enabling feature transfer without domain adaptation. We instantiate this approach as PACE , a Proprioception-

Anchored Cross-modal Encoder. Each modality-specific stream applies a learnable, per-dimension, zero-sum temporal kernel initialized to compute a first-order difference. The kernel suppresses static offsets and extracts a compact mo-tion code. During pretraining, a cycle-consistency objective anchors these codes to proprioception, while auxiliary objec-tives encourage complementary information across modali-ties. We then freeze the encoder, train an RL policy on its features, and deploy the resulting policy on hardware without adaptation. We evaluate PACE on four contact-rich assembly tasks with complementary challenges: PegInsert and GearMesh from the Factory benchmark, and JointAssemble (a square plug with rotational constraints) and KnobTighten (a knob-and-socket task with sloped contact geometry and increased reliance on F/T feedback) from the AutoMate dataset. Across these tasks, PACE achieves a 93.3% average real-world success rate, with an average sim-to-real decrease of 2.7 percentage points. Pose-based and learned-fusion baselines exhibit substantially larger decreases on hardware. The main contributions of this work are as follows:  

> •

We introduce proprioceptive anchoring , which uses proprioceptive state-transition prediction to promote domain-invariant visual and F/T representations without real-robot training data.  

> •

We propose learnable, zero-sum temporal differencing 

to suppress static offsets, together with cross-modal masked reconstruction to encourage complementary representations.  

> •

We demonstrate zero-shot sim-to-real deployment on four contact-rich assembly tasks from the Factory and AutoMate benchmarks, achieving a 93.3% average real-world success rate and a 2.7-percentage-point average decrease from simulation. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Contact-Rich Robotic Assembly 

Classical assembly methods rely on engineered com-pliance [8], [9], hybrid force–position control [10], or impedance control [11]; these approaches often require task-and geometry-specific design. Reinforcement learning has also been applied to contact-rich manipulation in simulation and on hardware [12]–[14]. The Factory benchmark [15] provides standardized assembly tasks in Isaac Gym, with privileged simulation states used as policy inputs in reported experiments. For hardware deployment, IndustReal [1] com-bines Mask R-CNN object detection with AprilTag-based camera calibration to estimate planar part poses (x, y, θ )

for a proximal policy optimization (PPO) policy. Under tight clearances, however, estimation errors propagate di-rectly to the controller. FORGE [16] and CoRMA [17] demonstrate that force-guided exploration can improve ro-bustness in contact-rich tasks, although neither method uses vision. AutoMate [2] scales to 100 assembly geometries and demonstrates zero-shot transfer to hardware, but its policies also rely on perception-initialized pose estimates. Residual RL methods [18] initialize policies through behavior cloning from real human demonstrations before refinement in simu-lation and therefore require real-robot data. By comparison, PACE enables pixel-to-action transfer without an external pose estimator or object tracker. 

B. Sim-to-Real Transfer 

Bridging the simulation-to-reality gap remains a central challenge in robot learning. Domain randomization [3], [4], [19], [20] samples visual and physical parameters during training. Although it can produce robust policies, residual errors may remain unacceptable at submillimeter tolerances. Domain adaptation aligns feature distributions across do-mains [5], [6], but requires real-robot data. System identifica-tion [7] calibrates simulation parameters to match hardware and is typically costly and task-specific. A third paradigm is asymmetric training, in which the critic or teacher receives privileged information while the actor or student receives only deployable observations [21], [22]. Our encoder-guided RL method follows this design: the actor receives encoder features, whereas the critic also observes privileged simulation state. In contrast to methods that rely on RL alone to learn invariant features, PACE pretrains the encoder explicitly for cross-domain consistency. PACE is complementary to these paradigms. Temporal differencing suppresses static offsets, and proprioceptive anchoring encourages the features to encode physical motion rather than domain-specific appearance. C. Representation Learning for Robotic Manipulation 

General-purpose visual encoders, including R3M [23], VIP [24], and VC-1 [25], are trained on large-scale datasets but are not designed specifically for the fine-grained spatial reasoning required in submillimeter assembly. DINOv2 [26] is a self-supervised vision transformer [27] that exhibits spatial correspondence; we use it as a frozen visual back-bone. Our cross-modal reconstruction objective draws on masked autoencoding [28] and contrastive representation learning [29]–[31]. Multimodal policies commonly use feature concatena-tion [32], [33] or cross-attention [34]. Tactile-only ap-proaches [35], [36] show that force sensing can support insertion when combined with a suitable search strategy, but provides limited information for initial alignment, where vision remains important. Dynamics-aware pretraining is more closely related to our setting. DynaMo [37] uses forward and inverse dynamics ob-jectives for visuomotor control but considers only visual ob-servations. AFRO [38] extends dynamics-aware pretraining to 3-D point clouds through feature differencing and inverse consistency. Although AFRO also uses temporal differences, it does not ground them in proprioception. MSDP [39] jointly pretrains visual, F/T, and proprioceptive representations through masked autoencoding and cross-sensor prediction and is the closest prior work to PACE. Whereas MSDP uses a shared transformer and a reconstruction objective, PACE uses modality-specific pathways, separate auxiliary losses, and proprioceptive anchoring. AugInsert [34] fuses vision and force through Perceiver IO but relies on behavior cloning without dynamics pretraining. Large vision–language–action models [40], [41] and diffusion policies [42] emphasize broad task coverage; PACE targets representations of fine-grained dynamics required for submillimeter assembly. PACE differs from prior multimodal pretraining in three respects. First, it uses a separate temporal kernel for each modality and fuses the streams only at the bottleneck. Second, it applies structured auxiliary supervision: a spatial alignment objective supervises the visual stream, while an engagement objective supervises both streams. Third, cross-modal masked reconstruction with stop-gradient discourages redundant representations. This modular design also supports stream-level ablations that quantify the contribution of force sensing when visual information is limited. Recent studies use proprioception as a comparatively reli-able signal in other contexts. KiVi [43] treats proprioception as a stable locomotion backbone when visual observations degrade, and TNavRL [44] relates noisy visual streams to proprioceptive states through cross-modal transformers for zero-shot navigation. PACE instead uses proprioceptive-transition prediction as a direct supervisory signal to filter domain-specific information from visual and F/T representa-tions. III. P ROBLEM FORMULATION

A. Expert Demonstrations 

For each task, we train a privileged-state PPO policy [45] in Isaac Lab [46]. The policy observes ground-truth poses, velocities, and contact states and we train it using the default Factory domain-randomization settings [15] until it achieves near-perfect success. We roll out the stochastic expert to collect 300 trajectories per task. At each time step, we record images from both wrist cameras, a short history of wrist F/T measurements, joint positions and velocities, the executed action, and a binary engagement label obtained from simulated contact detection. We partition the resulting dataset into training and validation sets using an 80:20 split. 

B. Encoder Architecture 

Each modality follows the same temporal-differencing pipeline: encoding each frame, buffering the preceding T

features, extracting a motion code with a learnable temporal kernel, and fusing the result with proprioception to produce the stream output. The temporal kernel is a per-dimension depthwise 1-D convolution initialized to approximate a first-order difference, [−1, 1, 0, . . . ]. On every forward pass, we subtract the per-channel mean of the weights to enforce an exactly zero-sum kernel. This constraint removes constant offsets, such as static appearance components or sensor bias, from the motion code and retains temporal changes in the visual and force signals. For the visual stream, a frozen backbone encodes images from both wrist cameras. We train two prediction heads using state labels that are readily available in simulation. Conditioned on the visual representation and previous action, the spatial alignment head predicts the normalized planar vector from the end effector to the target keypoint; for JointAssemble, it additionally regresses the yaw error. The spatial alignment loss Lalign uses a cosine loss on the planar direction whenever the target displacement has a well-defined direction and a smooth-L1 loss on yaw when applicable. Leng . These heads provide complementary supervision for spatial alignment and contact engagement, respectively. 

Cross-Modal Reconstruction. To discourage redundant stream representations, we apply masked autoencoding in the feature space. We uniformly mask half of the dimensions in the concatenated vector [zvt , zft , pt] and use a shallow MLP to reconstruct the complete vector. The reconstruction loss includes the visible dimensions, and the target is de-tached from the computation graph. Gradients therefore pass through the reconstructor to the visual and force pathways but not through the target. Locally correlated dimensions can be recovered within a modality, whereas the remaining information must be inferred across modalities. This objec-tive consequently encourages the two pathways to encode complementary information; its contribution is evaluated by the ‘w/o Masked Reconstruction’ ablation in Table I. 

Pretraining Procedure. The total pretraining loss is a weighted sum of the three objectives. We apply color jitter, blur, and cropping to the images, and add annealed Gaussian noise to the F/T measurements to approximate real sensor noise. We train the encoder with early stopping for a max-imum of 100 epochs. The four loss weights and ablation configurations are provided in the accompanying material. 

D. Policy Training and Deployment 

We freeze the pretrained encoder and use its features as observations for RL policy training. The encoder runs online at every simulation step and maintains the same temporal feature buffers used during pretraining. We employ an asymmetric actor–critic architecture: the actor observes only the latent features, proprioception, and the previous action, whereas the critic also receives privileged simulation state for value estimation. Thus, the actor depends only on observations available during deployment. We train all RL policies for 200 iterations in Isaac Lab using the same pol-icy architecture, hyperparameters, and domain-randomization settings, thereby isolating the effect of the observation rep-resentation. For deployment, we transfer the frozen encoder and policy to the robot without parameter updates or real-world training data. Camera images and F/T measurements undergo the same preprocessing used in simulation, and the encoder updates its temporal buffers online. A. Tasks and Evaluation 

We evaluate PACE on four contact-rich assembly tasks that span complementary geometric and contact-sensing chal-lenges. Two tasks are adapted from the Factory bench-mark [15]. PegInsert requires insertion of a 16-mm peg into a receptacle with approximately 0.1-mm radial clearance and primarily tests fine visual alignment. GearMesh requires a 30-mm-diameter gear to mesh with adjacent fixed gears and tests contact-sensitive translational alignment. The other two tasks are drawn from the AutoMate dataset [2]. JointAssemble (ID 00471) uses a 2×1×3.3-cm square plug that requires both translational and yaw alignment during insertion. Its auxiliary spatial alignment head therefore predicts planar direction and yaw error. 

KnobTighten (ID 00855) uses a 3.7×3.7×6.5-cm knob and a sloped socket whose contact geometry increases the importance of F/T feedback during engagement. Fig. 3 shows the robot setup and representative scenes for all four tasks. All experiments use a Franka FR3 manipulator equipped with two 224 × 224 -pixel wrist-mounted RGB cameras [47] and a six-axis wrist F/T sensor. At 15 Hz, the policy controls three-dimensional position and yaw, yielding a four-degree-of-freedom (4-DoF) action space; the controller fixes roll and pitch. An episode is successful if the assembly reaches the target pose within the task-specific tolerance. In simulation, we evaluate each task over 100 episodes with domain randomization and report the mean success rate and standard deviation over three random seeds. For real-robot evaluation, we deploy one fixed checkpoint for each method—task pair. We conduct 30 consecutive trials for each method–task pair, randomizing the initial gripper and target poses before every trial. After each trial, we record the outcome and randomize the setup for the subsequent trial. Success is determined from the final pose and contact conditions rather than subjective assessment. B. Baselines 

We compare PACE against three baselines representing privileged-state control, direct policy learning from un-adapted sensor features, and learned multimodal fusion. 

Pose-Based PPO. This baseline uses the privileged-state PPO policy described in Sec. IV. In simulation, the policy receives ground-truth object poses, keypoint relations, and contact variables. During deployment, the policy weights remain fixed, and unavailable geometric quantities are re-placed with estimates from an external perception module following IndustReal [1]. Object detection and AprilTag-based camera calibration provide planar part poses (x, y, θ ).This baseline represents a conventional modular deployment in which errors at the perception–control interface contribute to the sim-to-real performance decrease. 

No-Pretrain RL. The actor receives the concatenation of frozen DINOv2 visual features, the F/T history, propri-oception, and the previous action. This baseline omits task-specific encoder pretraining, proprioceptive anchoring, and masked reconstruction. We initialize the policy randomly and train it with PPO using the same RL budget as PACE. The comparison evaluates whether generic visual features and raw multimodal observations are sufficient to learn transferable assembly policies. 

AugInsert. We implement AugInsert using a Perceiver IO architecture [48]. DINOv2 patch tokens and F/T embeddings receive modality-specific positional encodings and are fused through cross-attention [34]. The model is first trained via behavior cloning using the expert demonstrations collected for PACE and is then fine-tuned with PPO under the same RL budget. All methods use the same expert data, RL budget, and real-robot evaluation protocol. This baseline evaluates whether behavior-cloning initialization and cross-attention-based multimodal fusion are sufficient for robust sim-to-real transfer. 

C. Ablations 

We evaluate four ablations to quantify the contributions of proprioceptive anchoring, force information, visual infor-mation, and masked latent reconstruction. Unless otherwise stated, the data, optimization settings, policy architecture, and RL budget remain fixed. 

Without Proprioceptive Anchoring ( Ldyn ). This variant sets the weight of Ldyn to zero while retaining the spatial alignment, engagement, and reconstruction objectives. It measures the contribution of proprioceptive-transition pre-diction to cross-domain transfer. 

Without the Force Stream. We remove the force rep-resentation, such that the policy observes [zvt , pt, at−1].This variant measures the contribution of F/T information, particularly in tasks involving meshing, rotational mismatch, or force-sensitive engagement with sloped geometry. 

Without the Visual Stream. We remove the visual repre-sentation, such that the policy observes [zft , pt, at−1]. This variant evaluates whether force and proprioception alone provide sufficient spatial information for initial alignment.

Removing masked reconstruction reduces performance un-der each perturbation, with the largest decreases under the lighting and background changes. These results indicate that proprioceptive anchoring and masked reconstruction jointly improve robustness to visual and F/T perturbations. VII. D ISCUSSION 

The results show complementary roles for the main com-ponents of PACE. Removing the dynamics loss causes the largest transfer degradation, indicating that proprioceptive anchoring is the primary contributor to cross-domain gen-eralization. The modality ablations show that vision is nec-essary for initial spatial alignment, whereas F/T sensing is particularly important for tasks involving meshing or other force-sensitive contact geometries. Masked reconstruction primarily improves robustness to appearance and sensor perturbations. Temporal differencing further complements these components by suppressing static offsets in the visual and F/T signals. VIII. C ONCLUSION 

We presented PACE, a proprioception-anchored cross-modal encoder for zero-shot sim-to-real transfer in contact-rich assembly. Across four tasks from the Factory and Auto-Mate benchmarks, policies trained on frozen PACE features transfer to hardware with substantially less performance degradation than policies based on pose estimates or learned multimodal fusion, while remaining robust to visual and sensor perturbations. The ablations highlight the complementary roles of the main components: proprioceptive anchoring promotes cross-domain generalization, vision supports spatial alignment, F/T sensing resolves contact-sensitive interactions, and masked reconstruction improves robustness when individual obser-vations become unreliable.
