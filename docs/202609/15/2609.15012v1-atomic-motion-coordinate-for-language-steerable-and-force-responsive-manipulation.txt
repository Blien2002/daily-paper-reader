Atomic Motion Coordinate for Language-Steerable and
Force-Responsive Manipulation
Jiaqi Zhai, Jingkai Zhao, Chen Yang, Siyuan Ma, Yutian Zhang, Liwen Yang,
Qinglian Wu, Weiqi Fan, Yifei Wang, Yi Zheng, Chenxi Gu, Dong Wei†, Wei Zhang†
Abstract— Can changing only the language instruction redi-
rect a vision-language-action (VLA) policy’s end effector, or
does the visually driven motion prior dominate? We present
AMC (Atomic Motion Coordinate), a geometry-grounded co-
ordinate for steerable and force-responsive manipulation. Each
arm owns thirteen signed translation, rotation, and hold atoms
grounded from text and forward kinematics with vision with-
held, and the coordinate is injected into every action-expert
block via a cross-attention residual. Contact history modulates
the same coordinate through a bounded spherical residual that
is recomputed from a fixed nominal latent to regenerate only the
unexecuted horizon suffix. Across 7,520 offline horizon interven-
tions, opposite-atom separation reaches 92.5/83.1% (single/dual)
versus 39.1/24.0% for LA4VLA-style. Across 50 real-robot trials
per task, AMC raises out-of-distribution (OOD) fruit progress
from 60.5% to 87.8%; force adaptation raises Plug/Vase from
59.0/71.6% to 78.5/75.2%.
I. INTRODUCTION
Generalist vision–language–action (VLA) policies such as
OpenVLA, π0, and π0.5 turn an instruction and a camera
stream into continuous control, and they succeed on a widen-
ing range of manipulation tasks [1], [2], [3]. The language
channel itself, however, is rarely measured. Demonstrations
tightly correlate what is said, what is seen, and what is done,
so a policy can reach a high success rate while treating
the instruction as a weak prior on an otherwise visually
driven routine. VIP shows that policies more readily acquire
task targets from visual specifications than from text, and
proposes sparse point flows as a more learnable instruction
channel [4]. Rather than replacing language, we ask what it
controls. With the same observation and robot state, does
changing only the instruction move the predicted end
effector in the requested direction rather than continue
the demonstrated motion?
Answering this requires a matched protocol in which
observation, robot state, and every inference setting are
frozen and only the prompt varies. Under such a proto-
col a fine-tuned π0.5 does respond—pretraining supplies
common-object semantics and our demonstrations supply ac-
tion support—but counterfactual trajectories can stay weakly
separated, or bend through the native-task direction before di-
verging. Responsiveness is present; directional reliability and
compositionality are not. Existing remedies act on the data,
through counterfactual relabeling, or on the representation,
by steering internal activations, yet the instruction remains an
implicit condition whose motor meaning is left unnamed [5],
[6], [7], [8], [9].

We therefore give language an explicit destination. AMC
(Fig. 1) inserts a bilateral motion coordinate between the
planner and the continuous action expert. Each arm owns a
spherical codebook of thirteen named atoms: signed transla-
tions, signed rotations, and hold. The coordinate is trustwor-
thy because of how it is grounded. A first phase withholds
all RGB and exposes only atomic text and normalized joint
state, so the only route to predicting the masked 50-step
action flow and its low-frequency structure is the atom
named in the prompt. Atom identity is anchored to forward-
kinematics (FK) geometry and cannot be explained away
by object appearance. Once vision is restored, each arm
emits a motion latent whose direction stays aligned with
this coordinate while a bounded tangent component restores
scene-specific detail. The ordered right/left pair is read by
all 18 action-expert blocks through an independent, zero-
initialized cross-attention path, leaving the unmodified self-
attention and flow-time conditioning intact so that in-domain
competence is preserved by construction. The planner it-
self outputs neither atoms nor weights: via a fixed prompt
template, it selects a subtask from the predefined subtask
vocabulary given the current visual observation. The low-
level policy maps that subtask and observation to per-arm
motion directions, each trained to align with FK-grounded
atoms, which the action expert uses as explicit conditioning
when generating the trajectory.
Contact-rich phases need feedback faster than a semantic
model can supply, and we express that feedback in the
same coordinate instead of a parallel one. A shared encoder
converts one second of wrench and state history into 30
slow tokens and the newest post-anchor samples into 10 fast
tokens; the nominal zM, augmented with the current real-
time chunking (RTC) phase [10], queries this memory to
produce a bounded tangent update on the unit sphere. Each
update regenerates only the unexecuted suffix of the current
50-step horizon and is recomputed from the same fixed zM
rather than accumulated, so the atom that named the motion
remains the anchor throughout contact.
Two boundaries scope our claim: we do not argue that
a strong baseline ignores language, nor that an explicit
coordinate can ground concepts absent from both pretraining
and robot data. Throughout this paper, OOD denotes held-out
recombinations of known targets, orders, and scene configu-
rations. Within that scope, AMC raises opposite-atom separa-
tion from 39.1/24.0% to 92.5/83.1% for single/dual prompts
over 7,520 matched offline interventions from recorded real-
robot states, while retaining closed-loop competence that

II. RELATED WORK
A. Language-Conditioned VLA and Latent Actions
OpenVLA predicts discretized actions from a vision-
language backbone, whereas π0 and π0.5 attach a con-
tinuous flow action expert to a pretrained vision-language
model (VLM) [1], [2], [3], continuing the broader use of
chunked, receding-horizon prediction in ACT and Diffusion
Policy [11], [12]. Action abstractions have been learned by
residual vector quantization, frequency-domain tokenization,
and visual future prediction [13], [14], [15], and LARY
argues that semantic decodability and physical reconstruction
must be scored separately [16]. Our codebook differs in
origin: it begins from an embodiment-specific geometric
vocabulary rather than a learned token inventory, while the
deployed policy still emits continuous trajectories.
Closest to us, LA4VLA removes vision during language–
action
pretraining,
then
returns
to
conventional
full-
observation VLA training [6]. It changes the curriculum but
retains no fixed FK-derived dictionary, per-arm intervention
variable, or contact-update site. We instead ground signed
geometric axes, preserve their coordinates under full observa-
tion, and expose them directly to the action expert. Residual
Semantic Steering (RSS) [17] combines Monte Carlo Syntac-
tic Integration with Residual Affordance Steering to amplify
language over a vision-only prior, but operates at the action-
distribution level without a named per-arm coordinate. CAST
and CofactVLA use counterfactual relabeling [5], [18]; other
work steers internal activations or filters plans through latent-
aligned VLM verification [7], [8]. None of these methods
exposes a fixed, per-arm FK-grounded coordinate shared by
language intervention and force adaptation.
B. Hierarchical Planning and Fast Contact Feedback
Hierarchical policies ground language into executable
skills and revise plans across stages [19], [20]. We keep
completed-state information in a compact recursive summary
from Qwen3.5-4B [21], a boundary-rate planner rather than
a controller: only its subtask reaches the motor policy.
On the contact side, RDP, ForceVLA, and FAVLA pair
a slow visuomotor route with faster tactile or force condi-
tioning [22], [23], [24], AT-VLA and T-Rex route native-
rate signals through specialized action paths [25], [26], and
predictive models forecast tactile evolution before revising
actions [27], [28]. FACTR shows that visually dominant poli-
cies can underutilize force without an explicit force-attending
curriculum [29]. We instead place the force correction in
the same spherical space as language, recomputed at every
update from one fixed nominal latent.
III. METHOD
AMC consists of three functional components, whose
low-level representation is trained progressively through
atomic grounding, full-observation continuation, and force-
adaptation stages. A planner turns the global task into one
executable subtask at a time (Sec. III-A). A per-arm atomic
coordinate is grounded from language and proprioception
with vision withheld, then reused under full observation to
condition every depth of the action expert (Sec. III-B–III-
D). A spherical force update revises that coordinate during
contact without displacing its semantic anchor (Sec. III-E).

IV. EXPERIMENTS
A. Platform, Data, and Four Task Families
Experiments use a CR1 dual-arm platform with two seven-
DoF arms, two grippers, a base camera, two wrist cameras,
and a six-axis force/torque sensor at each wrist. RGB, state,
and actions are recorded at 30 Hz; wrench is recorded at
120 Hz. FK annotation, evaluation, and visualization use the
same URDF and 0.20-m TCP. The unified training corpus
contains 2,375 episodes and 3.07M valid 30-Hz training
samples across cabinet, drawer, fruit, mixed manipulation,
screwdriver, plug, and vase domains. Normalization statistics
use the training split only. On a single RTX 4090, atomic-
only inference takes 117.4 ms; the force adapter runs in
118.7 ms in the slow loop (full suffix) and 45.5 ms in the
fast loop (partial suffix), both within the 333 ms ten-action
window at 30 Hz.
Figure 2 shows the four evaluation families. Multi-fruit
sorting measures per-subtask execution under manually se-
lected subtask prompts, with fruit positions randomized
across familiar configurations. Drawer retrieval and plug
insertion (hereafter Plug; Table II) requires opening the
drawer, grasping and reorienting the power adapter, aligning
the connector, and completing a contact-rich insertion, with
execution subtasks supplied by the Qwen3.5-4B planner.
OOD fruit ordering uses the same manual-prompt protocol
with unseen fruit placement and target-box positions. Biman-
ual vase wiping (Table II) alternates stabilization, rotation,
and sustained wiping contact, with subtasks supplied by the
Qwen3.5-4B planner.
B. Baselines and Metrics
We compare six policies: fine-tuned π0.5, matched repro-
ductions of LA4VLA [6] and ForceVLA [23], an adapta-
tion of RSS [17] to our real-robot setting, our force-free
AMC, and AMC + Force. All reproductions use the same
three-view RGB observations, 16-D dual-arm state, 32-D
action interface, and evaluation protocol; training subsets are
matched to each method’s evaluated task families. For real-
robot task progress, LA4VLA and RSS are evaluated only
on fruit tasks; ForceVLA only on Plug and Vase.
The primary robot metric is mean normalized task
progress. Each method is evaluated in 50 trials per task.
If trial i earns si out of the task’s prespecified maximum
S, its progress is 100si/S. Plug progress uses four one-
point macro-stages: opening the drawer, establishing the
grasp on the adapter, aligning the connector, and completing
insertion. The planner may issue multiple finer-grained ex-
ecution subtasks within each scored stage. Vase uses five
points: two for rotation (one per half-turn, stabilization
subsumed), two for wiping (one per half-clean), and one
for returning the vase. Scoring and termination conditions
are fixed across methods. For fruit tasks, a human operator
supplies the execution-subtask prompt at each boundary;
for Plug and Vase, Qwen3.5-4B generates subtask prompts
automatically. The two column groups therefore measure
distinct capabilities and are not pooled. Atomic steerability
follows the matched protocol of Sec. IV-D. Without task-
specific SFT, Qwen3.5-4B is evaluated on 500 Vase and 500
Plug semantic-boundary cases; at each, the planner is queried
at 1 Hz with the updated observation, its memory anchor,
and the previous subtask. A case succeeds if the correct next
subtask appears immediately or within five queries; success
is 94.6% (Vase) and 92.4% (Plug). These are boundary-level
scores.

V. LIMITATIONS AND CONCLUSION
The thirteen atoms encode dominant base-frame motion,
not grasp types, affordances, or complete symbolic programs;
object-centric vocabularies may suit other tasks, and FK
labels inherit calibration error. The method cannot ground
concepts absent from both pretraining and demonstrations.
The training-only dT teacher stabilizes zM, while force
conditioning modulates a joint-space generator rather than
a hybrid force–position controller. Planner reliability is mea-
sured at subtask boundaries rather than end to end.
Within these limits, AMC converts existing semantic
and action support into named per-arm motion coordinates
consumed throughout the action expert and revised by a
bounded, non-accumulating contact update. This yields di-
rectional, compositional language control and force feedback
in the same interpretable representation—a useful interface
for auditing and steering pretrained generalist policies.
